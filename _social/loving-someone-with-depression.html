Loving someone with depression when you also have depression isn’t as romantic as most people think.

When two depressives meet, the cliché is that they understand each other better. And that’s partially true, but it’s also partially a trap. The understanding is real. You know the difference between “I don’t want to talk to you” and “I don’t have anything to give right now, and I don’t want you to mistake my emptiness for something personal.”

But what’s harder is that depression eats reciprocity.

Not love.

Reciprocity.

The ability to return at the same temperature is one of the first things depression takes, and it’s also one of the things love is most often measured by. So when you’re low, you need a steady incoming signal to stay tethered, but you can’t always produce one yourself. And when they’re low, the same is true for them.

So the math of two people both running on a deficit is that whoever is slightly less depleted in that moment ends up carrying the thread.

And that person notices. And they can try to explain it away:

“They’re grieving.”
“They have a hangover.”
“They have a lot of work to do.”
“Their cat just died.”
“They’re overwhelmed.”
“They’re empty.”
“They’re doing their best.”

But the registry still happens somewhere in the back of the mind. And it accumulates.

The trap inside the trap is that the carrying starts to dress itself up as devotion. You tell yourself, “I’m okay being the one who reaches, because I love them and they’re having a hard time.”

But then you’re pouring from an empty cup and calling it patience. And eventually, the question starts forming:

Who fills you back up when you’re the one always trying to keep the connection alive?

That’s where the guilt creeps in, because you know they don’t mean to neglect you. You know they’re not choosing to be empty. But knowing that doesn’t make your own needs disappear.

And how long can you keep being the understanding one before you start needing someone to understand you back?
