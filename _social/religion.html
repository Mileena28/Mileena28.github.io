I was raised in a cult.

I have a lot of religious trauma, particularly surrounding Christianity. I’ve spent years working through it, and I’ve reached a point where I no longer resent religion or the people who practice it.

But when someone tells me I need to go to church, find God, or accept Jesus into my life, I wish they understood how tone-deaf that can be to someone with my experiences.

I’m not saying this to be abrasive or disrespectful. I just wish people would step outside their own perspectives for a moment and recognize that not everyone has experienced faith the same way they have.

Being religious does not automatically make you a good person.

Going to church every Sunday doesn’t make you a good person. Memorizing scripture or quoting the Bible better than someone else doesn’t make you a good person.

It’s how you treat people that matters.

You can preach about the love of Christ all day long, but if you aren’t practicing the compassion, kindness, humility, and empathy that he taught, then what are you actually accomplishing?

I believe this so strongly that it’s a recurring theme throughout my books.

In The Chosen, Lucien genuinely believes he’s saving the people of Arcadia by bringing them under one religion. These are people who have lost their homes, their loved ones, and their hope for the future. Their gods have abandoned them, and they’re desperate for something to believe in.

Lucien offers them hope, stability, and purpose. He’s admired and respected by his community. To many people, he’s their salvation.

But behind that image, he sexually abuses his wife, Mira, murders those who oppose him, and uses religion to manipulate and control the very people he claims to be saving.

And the frightening thing is that he believes he’s justified.

Similarly, in The Silence, Mordain is a High Inquisitor who has devoted his life to studying sacred texts and serving his religious institution.

He believes the Bloodmarked, people born with divine blood, are a corruption that must be eliminated. He imprisons, persecutes, and kills them in the name of righteousness.

He’s educated, devoted, and convinced he’s carrying out a sacred duty. But his faith has become a justification for cruelty rather than a reason to practice compassion.

Both characters illustrate something I feel strongly about: Believing you’re doing God’s work doesn’t automatically mean you’re doing something good.

I have absolutely no problem with people loving their God, practicing their religion, or finding comfort and purpose in their faith. I think it’s wonderful when someone’s beliefs help them become a kinder, more compassionate person.

But please remember that everyone has their own experiences, struggles, and reasons for believing what they believe.

And maybe, just maybe, there’s more than one path toward living a meaningful, compassionate life.

You don’t have to agree with someone’s beliefs to respect them. You don’t have to understand their experiences to acknowledge that those experiences matter.

Be fervent in your faith, but don’t become so consumed by being right that you forget how to be kind.

Just something I’ve been thinking about. And I genuinely say all of this with love.
