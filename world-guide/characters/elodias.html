The Moon That Chases the Sun

The ocean-bound bards who still sing to the dusk carry his story as a fragmented, mournful song, older—they claim—than the city of Atheria itself, from the time when the land was still called Quenos, a quiet village by the sea where gods once walked beside mortals. The melody shifts from coast to coast, but the names never change: Elodias, god of the moon and tides, and Senia, goddess of the sun, whose love the Historian Tanneus G'Raja calls the most tender myth in his collection, and the most tragic.

He is one of the Wild Gods—those who feel as deeply as any mortal despite their cosmic power—and he wears mortal shape well. In the stone-paved market square of Quenos he passed for a man, if a remarkable one: tall and fair-haired, with eyes the precise blue of a cloudless summer sky, his smiles heavy and his attention never truly engaged by the women who approached him. Some called him proud. Others speculated that he nursed a broken heart. None of them were right. For three weeks he watched a single stall from the shadow of an alley, from beside the baker's shop, from the well, from among the fishermen unloading their catch, before he bought a peach and spoke to the auburn-haired woman who sold it, with nothing in his casual greeting to suggest the weight of centuries behind it.

What binds him is a curse unlike any other: to find his beloved Senia in every incarnation, to love her for seven perfect days, and to lose her when the sun sets on the seventh night. She is reborn mortal, without memory; he keeps his immortality and his perfect recall, and finds her again. Yet neither divine decree nor mortal death can extinguish the flame they carry for each other across the ages. Every morning he came to her stall in Quenos, he came knowing exactly what it would cost.

Why would I look at the stars, when I have always loved the sun?
— Elodias to Senia, at her stall in the market of Quenos (Seven Days of Sun, Chapter 1)

Divine Heart, Mortal Weakness

The Historian's readers meet him as a patient, teasing courtier before they meet him as a god. He leans his elbows on a fruit stall with casual grace, threatens to report a three-copper peach to "the peach council," pays a fourth copper as a "beauty tax," and lets his fingers linger against her palm a moment longer than necessary. Beneath the charm is a devotion so absolute that it has become the mechanism of his own punishment: the curse works because he cannot stay away.

Patient
He watches for weeks before he speaks, and lets each visit to her stall run a few words longer than the last. He carries the weight of centuries and spends it three coppers at a time.
Devoted Past Reason
He keeps a journal of her faces across lifetimes—one with sunlit curls, one veiled in market cloth, one crowned in blossoms—drawn, the Historian says, "as if remembered from dreams," with sun daisies pressed between the pages.
Grieving
His smiles are heavy even when he is playing. The Historian believes the salt-crystallised journal he found on the coast north of old Quenos holds "the grief of a god who remembers every life she lived, and every time she loved him—before the end came again."
Bound to the Sea
His moods and the water are not separate things. When his heart turns, the tide answers; the first chapter of his tale ends with a wave crashing "a little too hard against the shore."
Always. In every life, in every form. I will always find my way back to you, Senia. I'm not strong enough to stay away.
— Elodias, as the Historian's summary of the tale records him

Cosmic Power, Cosmic Prison

Elodias holds dominion over the moon and the tides, and the sorrowful depths of the sea are his to command. Yet all that power cannot touch the one thing that matters. The star-readers of the Almanac say the constellation called The Lovers—two lights circling eternally, one remembering, one forgetting—was written into the sky in the moment the curse took hold, and that Elodias chose eternal cycles of love and loss over never seeing Senia again.

The Eternal Pattern

Each incarnation follows the same arc. The Historian's readers see only its opening movement in the chapters now online—the market, the peach, the trade of fruit for an evening's walk—but the shape of the whole is written in the story's own summary: seven perfect days before fate claims its due.

The Seven Days of Sun
The Search
Somewhere in the world she is born again, and he feels the pull. He watches from a distance first—weeks of it, in Quenos—telling himself he will only look.
The Finding
"Morning, sunshine." A peach, three coppers, a beauty tax. He courts her patiently, without haste, until she asks the question that has been quietly surfacing: Why me?
The Trade
"A trade. The peach… for a date." A walk on the shore path when the sun starts to set. "Don't bring anything. Just you." She feels something beginning—something that tastes like love and danger all at once.
The Seven Days
Seven perfect days of love, and then the sun sets on the seventh night. How remembrance comes to her, and what it costs, is the heart of the tale.
After
Grief, and the sea that answers it. Then, somewhere, a child is born—and the god who is not strong enough to stay away begins to search again.
A trade. The peach… for a date.
— Elodias, closing the bargain that begins every seven days (Seven Days of Sun, Chapter 1)

Love Eternal, Death Inevitable, Hope Unquenchable

Elodias is the moon that chases the sun. God of the moon, of quiet stillness, of the sea's patient, sorrowful depths, he was made to complement Senia's radiant warmth, and their love was the kind the bards of the coast still sing to the dusk. It became, through the jealousy of a lunar seer, the kind of love that is also a sentence: to find her in every incarnation, to love her for seven perfect days, and to lose her when the sun sets on the seventh night.

What makes his story the most tender in the Historian's collection is that he keeps choosing it. He is the one who remembers. He carries a journal of her faces across lifetimes and sun daisies pressed between its pages, and still he walks into the market of Quenos and asks for the best peach. The star-readers say the constellation called The Lovers was written into the sky in the moment he chose eternal cycles of love and loss over never seeing her again—two lights circling eternally, one remembering, one forgetting.

Whether that choice is his greatest devotion or his fatal weakness is the question the tale exists to ask, and its published chapters answer only with a wave that crashes a little too hard against the shore. Read Seven Days of Sun for the courtship, and The Weaver of Sorrows for the seer who made it a curse.

Witness the God Who Loves the Sun

Follow Elodias into the market of Quenos and out along the shore path at sunset. His story explores the dangerous beauty of a devotion that will not let go, and the price a god pays for loving a mortal.
