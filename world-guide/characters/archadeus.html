The Hunt Given Its Hunger

When the First shaped the world, Archadeus gave the hunt its hunger, the predator its purpose. He arrives in The God Who Wept as a bitter laugh cutting through the heavens and an air that thickens "with the scent of blood and wild spaces," bursting into the divine realm "in a rush of heat and barely contained violence." His form will not settle: wolf to bear to great hunting cat, "too wild, too fundamentally untamed to be contained in any single form." Steam rises from his words, carrying the metallic tang of fresh-spilled blood and the musk of creatures that have never known fear. When he finally holds a shape, it is something upright and still bestial—the head of a great silver wolf, "eyes like molten gold that had witnessed the birth and death of countless worlds."

His creations are the Beastmen: the feline tribes who hunt with perfect grace, the ursine clans whose strength moves mountains, the avian warriors who command the skies, and his lupine children, "hidden though they may be." To Archadeus they understand the fundamental truth of existence—that only the strong deserve to shape their fate—and his brother Thainos's soft-skinned humans, huddled around their pathetic fires and weeping when the rain touches them, are "prey animals playing at being predators." The first real winter, he predicts, will cull half of them. The first plague will scatter the rest.

He is remembered in the mortal world as the War God. In the Crimson Tundra of a much later age, the Bloodfang Clans of the Feline Beastmen still worship him through ritualized sacrifice, and a cub of unprecedented Bloodmarked potency named Ryx Shadowclaw learns what that worship costs. But the tale that introduces him allows him something more than appetite: when Thainos speaks of a mother lifting a fallen tree to reach her child, the war god's bestial form stills, and his golden eyes hold "a flicker of something that might have been understanding."

Your humans? They're prey animals playing at being predators. No claws to rend their enemies, no fangs to tear the throat from their prey. The first real winter will cull half of them. The first plague will scatter the rest.
— Archadeus, on the making of humanity (The God Who Wept, Chapter 1)

The Philosophy of Fang and Claw

Archadeus speaks the oldest law in creation as he understands it: the weak fall to the strong, "written in blood and bone before any of us drew breath." He is restless, prowling in a circle around his brother as he argues, snarling where Libros calculates and Atonia measures. He is also, in his way, honest—strength is a thing you can see, and he does not dress his contempt in equations. What he cannot see, Thainos tells him, is a strength that comes "not from muscle and bone, but from love that refused to accept defeat."

Master of the Hunt Eternal

Archadeus is the archetype of every predator, and every predator is in some measure his: he shaped the dynamics of hunter and hunted when reality first drew breath, and he shaped a people—the Beastmen—to embody them. His presence alone is a weapon; his forms are many; and his children, from the feline tribes to the hidden wolves, still carry the hunt's hunger into the ages after the gods fell silent.

The Doctrine of the Strong

Archadeus's creed is stated in a single scene of The God Who Wept, and answered there by his brother. It is not refuted. Thainos does not deny that the weak fall to the strong; he denies that fang and claw are the only strength. "I have seen the weak protect the strong, the small shelter the large, the mortal stand defiant before the eternal—not because they were powerful, but because they chose to be brave." Whether the war god ever came to agree is a matter for the tales that follow.

Pack and Pantheon

Archadeus's bonds are those of the First—siblings who each made a portion of the world and quarreled over the youngest's—and those of a maker to his made. His Beastmen carry his hunger; his worshippers in the far north carry his name; and the strongest of their cubs carry, in a later age, a divine mark that makes them prey to their own clans.

Strength, and What It Cannot See

Archadeus is the War God of the First—the one who gave the hunt its hunger and the predator its purpose, and shaped the Beastmen in the image of that purpose. He is heat and blood-scent and a shape that will not hold still, wolf and bear and hunting cat and at last a great silver wolf's head on an upright frame, with molten-gold eyes that have watched worlds be born and die. His judgment of humanity is the bluntest of the five that Thainos must answer: they are prey animals playing at predators, and the first winter will cull them.

The tale does not make him a fool. He speaks the oldest law in creation as he knows it, and Thainos does not deny that law—only that it is the whole of strength. And when the youngest god speaks of a mother lifting a fallen tree, the war god's form stills, and something that might have been understanding flickers in his eyes. It is the only crack the chapter shows in any of the First, and it belongs to the hunter.

His name outlives the age of the gods. In the Crimson Tundra the Bloodfang Clans still worship Archadeus the War God with ritualized sacrifice, and it is under his name that a Feline Beastman cub, Ryx Shadowclaw, is declared a sacred offering. What the War God made of a later age in which the hunted learned to hunt gods—the age of the Bloodmarked—is the matter of the anthology's final tale.
