A Storm Pretending to Be a Breeze

Princess Jade of Moradim arrives at the healer's gate "like a storm trying to pretend it was merely a breeze," in traveling clothes that try desperately to be simple and fail at every turn—the fabric too fine, the cut too precise, the color too perfectly matched to the warm undertones of her tanned skin. Her black hair is braided in what is clearly meant to be a practical style and still catches the light like polished obsidian. She is, she announces, traveling incognito, "blending in with the common folk, experiencing life beyond palace walls, very educational"—wearing enough jewelry to fund a small village for a year. She has opinions about window boxes. She thinks cascading petunias would be divine.

Beneath the practiced laugh and the constant chatter is a young woman who has not slept properly in two months. Every night she walks through a grey and dusty city that is her city and is not—empty streets, toys left in the dust, perfectly good books with their pages flapping, a woman screaming somewhere she can never find. Sometimes there is a man: a brooding prince with cheekbones you could cut glass with, whose eyes are actually black, "like looking into nothing," and shadow-things beside him that make people simply stop existing. Sleeping draughts made her fuzzy. Wine gave her a headache. Philosophy before bed only left her more confused about existence and still dreaming about abandoned cities. So she comes to the woman by the river that Countess Meredith told Lady Pemberton about, who told her friend Celia.

What Khali finds when she sings the princess to sleep and follows her down is no anxiety dream. It is prophecy—a Moradim of centuries hence, unmade by a hungry silence—and Jade, silly and vain and unexpectedly endearing, has the Sight. What she does with the friendship that grows out of that discovery, and how the world comes to remember her, is the tale the anthology's blurb calls The Song of Blood and Stone: "how wealth rewrites history."

Oh, your cottage is absolutely enchanting! It's exactly how I've always imagined a fairy tale dwelling should look—you know, the sort where the wise woman is secretly lovely and the woodland creatures all adore her.
— Jade, at Khali's gate (The Song of Blood and Stone, Chapter 2)

The Heart Beneath the Performance

Jade performs. Her laugh "had definitely been practiced in front of mirrors"; her mannerisms are cultivated; she can find the superficial even in cosmic horror ("Do you know what this is going to do to my social calendar?"). But intelligence flickers behind the affectation, and when the mask slips—hands trembling around a teacup, knuckles white at the memory of a rival's sincere apology—what shows is someone raw, frightened, and starved for a friend who values her for more than her bloodline. She has never gardened; "we have people for that." She turns out to be surprisingly good at following instructions when she wants to be.

Charmingly Relentless
Sweeps into a cottage without invitation, touches everything like a curious magpie, and keeps up a running commentary on skincare, seating charts and other people's scandals.
Frightened Underneath
"Please tell me this is just my mind being dramatic." Dark circles under expertly applied cosmetics; hands that shake when she is not actively controlling them.
Sharper Than She Lets On
"You walked into my dreams like it was nothing. You're not just some hedge witch, are you?" Her questions show a sharp mind beneath all that courtly training.
Eaten by Comparison
Treila glides through life "like it's all been arranged for her convenience." Jade knows it is petty. She spent three days trying to find a fabric the exact color of moonlight anyway.
I hate her. I know it's petty. I know it makes me a terrible person. But she glides through life like it's all been arranged for her convenience.
— Jade, of Treila, in Khali's garden

The Sight, the Crown, and the Story

Jade's gifts are a princess's gifts—gold coins pulled from a purse that cost more than the cottage, a palace with a breakfast room of crystal and silver, a court that will believe what it is told—and one that no one, least of all Jade, expected: the rare ability to see possible futures in her dreams. Khali cannot remove it; it is "part of you, woven into your very being." She can only build walls between the dreaming mind and the visions.

The Sight
Prophetic Dreams: An abandoned Moradim of generations hence, its architecture evolved beyond current city planning, dolls with clockwork eyes
The Black-Eyed Prince: A thing "that had crawled into human flesh and worn it like an ill-fitting costume," speaking of silence being hungry and the void missing its children
Awakened by Stress: The dreams began the night of the Midsummer Ball, and grew until she could taste ash and wake with her ears ringing
Muffled, Not Removed: Herbs from liminal places under her pillow, so that she may live her life and let the future worry about itself
Royal Standing
House of Moradim: Daughter of King Aldric, "weathered" by the burden of power, and Queen Marwyn, "the sharp beauty of a blade that had never been allowed to dull"
Court and Ladies: Celia, Countess Meredith, Lady Pemberton, the Summer Gala and the Harvest Ball; a great-aunt Cordelia in a portrait who murdered her husband, "but they could never prove it"
Wealth: Pays without haggling, in gold, and offers to get a hedge-witch "a proper dress. Something in jewel tones"
Alliance: Betrothed to a prince shipped across an ocean "to marry you for political reasons"
Performance

The Legend of High Pass

From the Gate to the Stones

The two chapters online end with Jade clutching a bundle of herbs and threatening to haunt Khali first if the world ends. What comes after—the friendship in the garden, the fiancé from Fayespire, the road to High Pass—is the rest of the tale, and its ending is the reason the Historian sat with this story "longer than any other" in his collection.

The Princess and the Prophecy
Royal Childhood
Raised in the palace of Moradim under a mother "suspicious of anyone who doesn't come with a verified genealogy going back at least ten generations" and a father who calculates dowries.
The Midsummer Ball
Treila arrives in the identical gown—hand-sewn pearl details, Ashanti silk from the eastern provinces—and apologizes sincerely. Everyone takes her side. The nightmares begin that night.
The Witch by the River
Two months sleepless and three jars of under-eye cream later, Jade finds Khali's gate. "You're just a very pretty woman who lives alone and makes tea."
The Sight Revealed
The herbs fail; the dreams sharpen; Khali walks into them and returns shaking. "Real," she says. Jade is a seer, and the empty city is a possible future.
Friendship in the Garden
Rosemary, lavender, feverfew "which despite its name is better for headaches than fevers." Borrowed work clothes, soil-stained fingers, and the first friend who is not an audience.
The Prince from Fayespire
Her betrothed arrives from across the ocean—"devastatingly handsome in that brooding, mysterious way that the eastern islanders do so well." Where that leads is the tale's to tell.
The Gilded Serpent
The anthology names her the Gilded Serpent, and its blurb calls her tale one of "how gold can transform villainy into heroism." Tanneus G'Raja accepted the mural's version for years, until Captain Morris's journal and a magistrate's sealed files showed him "blood patterns that didn't match testimonies," "defensive wounds on the wrong person," and reports that had been edited, revised, purchased. "The mural still stands," he writes, "and the official story continues to comfort those who need their darkness defeated and their Light victorious."
At this point, I'd let you paint me blue and dance naked under the moon if it would give me one decent night's sleep.
— Jade, before the dream-walking (The Song of Blood and Stone, Chapter 2)

Bonds of Court and Garden

Jade's world is the palace of Moradim—its portraits, its seating feuds, its ladies-in-waiting who pretend to sympathize—and, for a while, a garden by a river where nobody expects her to perform. "You're the only real friend I have," she tells Khali. What she does with that friendship is the whole of the tale.

Carved in Stone, Written in Gold

Princess Jade of Moradim is, on first meeting, a comedy: a socialite playing at incognito, dispensing decorating advice to a witch, comparing the horror of empty streets to being at court when everyone has gone to watch an execution. She is also a woman terrified of her own sleep, and the tale is careful to let both be true. Her nightmares are the first sighting in the anthology of the Silence that will one day come for Moradim, and it is Jade—not a priest, not a scholar—who sees it, centuries early, and cannot un-know it.

Her friendship with Khali is real. Weeks of pulling weeds and complaining about Treila give her the first relationship in her life that is not a performance, and she defends it at her own family's breakfast table with a warning in her voice that surprises everyone, including herself. Then her betrothed arrives from Fayespire, and the threads that Khali can feel drawing tight begin to close.

How the world remembers what happened at High Pass is public knowledge in Arcadia: a mural, a heroic legend, a brave princess. How the Historian came to doubt it—a captain's guilty conscience, a magistrate's sealed records, a purchased report—is the note that opens the tale. Tanneus G'Raja's own conclusion is bleaker than any mural: "History is not written by the victors. It is written by the wealthy." Jade of Moradim is the case that taught him so, and the reason he came to believe that "we are not a species that treasures truth. We are a species that treasures comfort."

Moradim itself would not keep its name; after the Divine War the land became Solara. But the stone sanctuary near what was once High Pass still stands, and the mural on its wall still tells the comfortable version.

Witness the Making of a Legend

Meet Jade at Khali's gate in the two chapters that are online, and read the Historian's note on the mural at High Pass. Her story reveals how friendship can turn, how power can rewrite truth, and how a lie carved in stone outlives the people it buries.
