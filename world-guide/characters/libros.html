The Equation Eternal

Before the world learned to name itself, the First Gods each claimed their portion of creation and shaped it according to their nature. Leika raised the mountains from her breath; Archadeus gave the hunt its hunger; Atonia distilled truth from chaos; Verites whispered darkness into being. And Libros inscribed the laws that held reality in place — "every angle perfect, every equation balanced." When Libros the Lawgiver manifests in the opening chapter of The God Who Wept, they come "not as flesh but as living mathematics — geometric perfection made manifest, angles and curves that existed in dimensions mortal minds could never perceive. They were the eternal constant, the unchanging foundation upon which all other realities rested."

To look upon Libros directly, the text says, is "to glimpse the underlying equations that held existence together — the precise calculations that kept stars in their courses, the immutable formulae that governed the dance of atoms." Even the other First Gods find their forms subtly adjusting in the Lawgiver's presence, "reality itself bending to accommodate the presence of absolute order." Their words resonate "through every plane of existence simultaneously," and around them appear golden threads — fate-lines and possibility streams, equations written in light that show the whole web of cause and effect.

Libros is the god of order among gods, and their creations reflect it. Where Thainos's humans would know "the chaos of longing," Libros's angels "knew only perfect order." When their youngest sibling shapes creatures of clay at the River of First Light and breathes questions into them instead of commands, it is Libros who processes the result through established probability matrices and reports what the numbers say. The numbers do not say anything kind.

They will stumble. Finite lifespan paired with infinite desire. Limited understanding burdened with unlimited choice. Processing these factors through established probability matrices...
— Libros, on the first humans (The God Who Wept, Ch. 1)

Pure Logic Incarnate

Libros argues from order. Freedom, to the Lawgiver, is a design flaw: a creation capable of failing is inferior to one that cannot. Their critique of humanity in the first chapter is not cruel in tone — it is a calculation, delivered with the certainty of a proof — and Thainos answers it at length.

Absolute Order
"The eternal constant, the unchanging foundation upon which all other realities rested." Reality bends to accommodate them.
Probability as Judgment
Human fate-threads "writhed and knotted, chaotic beyond calculation," where divine creations flow "in perfect, predictable patterns." The verdict follows from the model.
Pride in the Angels
"My angels move in perfect formation, each knowing their precise place in the cosmic symphony… They choose, yes — but they choose correctly, consistently, in perfect alignment with divine will."
Poetry Diagnosed as Collapse
"You have crafted instability itself, brother, and somehow convinced yourself it is beauty… You dress collapse in poetry and call it creation."
Their joys are quantifiably fleeting—I have calculated the precise half-life of their happiness. Their love is demonstrably unstable—I have seen the probability curves.
— Libros, to Thainos (The God Who Wept, Ch. 1)

The Argument from Order

Each of the First Gods who comes to challenge Thainos above the gathering where three rivers meet brings a different measure of worth. Libros's is predictability. The exchange in the first chapter is the clearest statement of it the guide can offer.

The Lawgiver in the First Chapter
Creation
Among the architect-gods who sang substance from the void, Libros claims law: every angle perfect, every equation balanced.
The Angels
Their creation — perfect formation, perfect harmony, perfect alignment with divine will. The counter-example to everything Thainos is about to make.
Manifestation
Before the echo of Leika's words has settled, "space itself began to organize around a new presence." Libros arrives as living mathematics.
The Calculation
Human threads writhe and knot beside the ordered golden patterns of angelic existence. "They will choose folly over wisdom, passion over logic, chaos over the divine order that sustains all existence."
The Answer
Thainos, "a smile that held infinite patience": "Yes, they are free — because only through freedom can they discover who they truly wish to become… A choice that cannot be unmade is not truly a choice at all — it is merely the elegant illusion of will."
The Verdict Stands
"Libros's geometric form pulsed with calculating light. 'The probability of their long-term success remains statistically negligible.'"
Afterward
The First Gods sit in judgment again in the Historian's later accounts; the Lawgiver's golden equations are among them when they do. What they rule on is the matter of The Divine War.
The Calculation Paradox
Libros can model everything except the thing Thainos is betting on. Their threads show what humans will probably do; they cannot show what a human might choose to become. Thainos's whole defence turns on that gap — "They are not weak. They are becoming." — and the Lawgiver, having no equation for becoming, answers with a probability. The chapter lets both stand.
The probability of their long-term success remains statistically negligible.
— Libros, after Thainos's defence (The God Who Wept, Ch. 1)

Logic Without Wisdom

Libros is the First God of order — the Lawgiver who inscribed the equations reality runs on and who appears, when they appear at all, as those equations made visible. In the opening chapter of The God Who Wept they are the second of the siblings to answer Thainos's creation, and their answer is the most complete: not disgust, not contempt, but a proof. Finite lifespan and infinite desire; limited understanding and unlimited choice; run the numbers, and humanity is instability itself.

What makes the Lawgiver more than a villain is that the chapter grants their premise. The angels really do sing in harmonies that keep the celestial spheres in motion; the human threads really do writhe and knot beyond calculation. Libros is not wrong about the data. Thainos's reply — that a choice which cannot be unmade is only "the elegant illusion of will," that there is "no virtue in obedience that has never been tested" — does not dispute the numbers. It disputes what the numbers are for.

The exchange is the anthology's first statement of a pattern it returns to: beings with power justifying their dominion over beings without it, each from their own nature. Libros justifies from order. Where that argument leads them, and what the First Gods do when they next sit in judgment, is the substance of the Historian's later accounts. Here, above the place where three rivers meet, the Lawgiver has the last word among the siblings, and it is a probability — "statistically negligible" — before Atonia steps forward and turns the argument into a wager.
