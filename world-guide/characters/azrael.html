From Heaven's Protector to the Demon in the Woods

Azrael was the God of Guardian Angels until the day he chose mercy over divine law, and he was cast from heaven for killing his corrupted brother. Now he walks the earth bearing the weight of necessary sin, and the people of Oakhaven, who have never heard his name, call him the forest demon.

In the Weathered Willow Inn they tell it over their ale. The devil in the Dark Forest has been there longer than anyone's gran's gran could remember. He never ages a day, never shows his face in decent company. He is tall as a tree and twice as dark, with eyes that glow like hellfire—"same cursed green eyes that see right through your soul," and the same description passed from mother to daughter for a hundred years or more. Girls go missing up and down the valley and are found only as pools of blood. Children sing a rhyme about him: The demon dwells in darkest wood / Where light fears to fall / He feasts on those who trust too much / And darkness takes them all.

The Historian Tanneus G'Raja followed that rhyme to a "hermit devil" in monastery archives three centuries old, to trade routes that merchants avoided through a "cursed forest," to a shrine in the hills whose lichen-covered inscription reads "For protection from angels," and at last to the rusted bloodstains on the floorboards of a cabin deep in the Dark Forest. "Sometimes," he concludes, "what we name as demons are simply those who choose mercy in a world that counts it as sin."

Are you going to be all right?
— The dark stranger to Rynn, before the darkness swallows him again (The Sin of Mercy, Chapter 1)

The Weight of Necessary Choices

The reader meets him not as a god but as a shape that crashes out of the dark into a corrupted beast "with a sound like thunder," and then as a man who looks away quickly, "as if trying to make some internal decision," when a wounded tavern girl thanks him. He sighs—"a sound full of weariness and resignation"—asks if she will be all right, and walks back into the forest depths without answering when she calls after him: Who are you?

Ancient Sorrow
His green eyes hold "depths of sorrow that seemed older than the forest itself." His voice is lower and rougher than Rynn expected. His weariness goes deeper than one night's fight.
Protector Still
Exiled from the office of protecting mortals, he protects them anyway—killing the beast that has already torn a boy apart, and taking its claws across his own chest without a sound.
Withdrawn
He does not give his name. He does not linger for thanks. He is gone "as completely as if he'd never been there at all," leaving only a dead beast and a spreading pool of lamp oil as proof.
Mercy as Sin
He chose mercy over divine law once and paid with heaven. In a small forest village, the tale's own summary promises, he discovers that redemption comes not from divine forgiveness but from the stubborn love of those who see past the monster to the man.
And sometimes, the greatest mercy is the one that damns us.
— Tanneus G'Raja, Author's Note to The Sin of Mercy

Grace Fallen Yet Purpose Found

Whatever the heavens stripped from him, they did not take his hands. Against a creature whose claws could have torn through stone and whose jaws could have crushed bone, "the man flowed around each attack like he could read the beast's thoughts," seized it by the throat and drove it backward into an oak with such force that the ancient trunk cracked, and put his sword through its chest in one fluid motion.

From Kinslayer to the Man Behind the Monster

The Historian calls his account "a haunting exploration of divine corruption, moral conviction, and the catastrophic cost of choosing love over duty." Its published chapter shows only the first meeting in the woods; the shape of the whole is in the tale's own summary.

Found in Blood
"I found this story in blood. Not metaphorically—though there is blood enough in the telling. I mean literally, in the rusted stains that still mark the floorboards of a cabin deep in what locals call the Dark Forest. The pattern of it troubles me even now. Too much for violence alone. Too deliberate for accident. As if something profound and terrible had occurred in that small space, leaving its signature in crimson."
Because blood on floorboards might tell a different story than the one we've inherited. Because sometimes what we name as demons are simply those who choose mercy in a world that counts it as sin.
— Tanneus G'Raja, Author's Note to The Sin of Mercy

Bonds Forged in Love and Loss

Azrael's bonds are few and heavy: the brother he killed, the heaven that cast him out, the village that fears him, and the stubborn tavern girl who walked into his woods with a lantern and refused to see a monster.

Grace Through Chosen Love

Azrael was the God of Guardian Angels until the day he chose mercy over divine law, killed his corrupted brother, and was cast from heaven. He is one of the Wild Gods, and he walks the earth bearing the weight of necessary sin: a hermit in a cabin deep in the Dark Forest, ageless, black-haired, green-eyed, whom the crossroads village of Oakhaven has sung into a children's rhyme and blamed for every girl who has ever gone missing in the valley.

The reader first sees him as the villagers never have: crashing out of the night to kill the corrupted beast that has just torn a boy apart, taking its claws across his chest without a sound, and then asking a wounded tavern girl whether she will be all right before vanishing without giving his name. It is Rynn's refusal to accept the tavern's version of him—her stubborn insistence that monsters don't save people—that begins the tale proper.

What the god of guardian angels chose in that small forest village, and what it cost him and everyone who loved him, is the subject of The Sin of Mercy. The Historian found the story in blood on a cabin floor, and offers only this by way of judgment: "sometimes the greatest mercy is the one that damns us." Nor is The Sin of Mercy the last the Historian has to say of him.
