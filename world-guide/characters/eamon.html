He Came Looking

Eamon enters The Divine War as a pair of strong arms wrapped around Astrid's waist from behind—"someone who smelled of sawdust and leather and mortal sweat"—lifting her off her feet and running while a god drags her friend away into the dark. He gets her home, slams the door, and holds it shut with surprising strength when she lunges for it. "There's nothing you can do now." He is Astrid's lover; the cottage is theirs; and his scarred face, harsh in the lamplight, shows a fear that is entirely for her.

He deals with terror by turning it to anger. The things he says that night are cruel and he knows it: that Natalia is already gone, that Astrid abandoned her the moment she stayed out past sunset, that "everything that happens to her now is your fault." Then the fight drains out of him all at once and he is on the bed beside her, holding her, apologizing into her hair, shaking as hard as she is. He came looking when she didn't come home. He thought he would find her dead. His prayer for Natalia—that she be "only" violated—is the kind of mercy his world has taught him to hope for.

He is the voice of what the village knows: the rules, the curfew, the plain truth that mortals are wheat before the scythe. Heroes, warriors, mages who could command the elements have tried to fight back—"Where are they now? Dead, or worse." When Astrid asks why they keep praying, he does not know. "Maybe because we're fools. Maybe because hope is all we have left, even when there's no reason for it." When a Trickster later comes to the forge where he works, what Eamon answers—and what it costs him—is the tale's to tell.

The Wild Gods are just stories, Astrid. Fairy tales we tell ourselves to make the darkness bearable. No one's coming to save us. No divine champion will descend from the heavens to protect the innocent. We're alone.
— Eamon to Astrid, the night Natalia was taken (The Divine War, Chapter 1)

The Strength That Holds the Door

Eamon is a practical man in an impractical horror. He knows what iron blades are worth against "creatures who were old when the mountains were young," and he knows what the gods do to those who resist: they make examples of them. His love shows in what he does—coming out into the dark after her, standing beside her at a funeral pyre without offering empty words—more than in what he says, because what he says when he is frightened tends to wound.

Fear Turned to Anger
"This was how Eamon dealt with terror—by turning it to anger." He accuses, then apologizes: "I'm so sorry. I was terrified when I realized you weren't home yet."
Clear-Eyed
"They are stronger, faster, immortal. We are wheat before the scythe." He has no comfortable illusions to offer, and refuses to invent any.
Steady Hands
Works iron by lamplight, keeps a friend's orders straight, and holds a shaking woman until the fear passes. Good, honest labor "that let a man's hands speak the language his heart sometimes couldn't find words for."
Keeper of the Rules
"Stay inside after dark. Don't draw their attention." Everyone knows the rules; Eamon believes in them because they are all a mortal has.
I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I was terrified when I realized you weren't home yet. When I saw you out there with that... thing... I thought I was going to lose you too.
— Eamon, into Astrid's hair (The Divine War, Chapter 1)

Mortal Hands, Mortal Heart

Eamon has no gift but his hands and his nerve, and the tale is at pains to show that these are not nothing—that they carried Astrid home when nothing else could have. Whatever else he is offered later, the chapter online shows only a man who runs toward the dark for someone he loves.

When the Trickster Comes to the Forge

The first chapter leaves Eamon and Astrid lying together in the darkness, too shattered for sleep, Eamon starting at every sound outside the window. The world guide's history records what came next for Astrid: a Trickster's offer of divine blood, and the first of the Bloodmarked. Eamon's part in that story—the same offer, made in a blacksmith's shop, and a different answer—is The Divine War's to tell.

The Choice at the Forge
The anthology's blurb promises "the origin of the Bloodmarked." Eamon is the man who was offered that origin and stands, in the tale, as the measure of Astrid's choice—the same gift, from the same god, and a different answer. Whether his answer was wisdom or only helplessness, and what it did to the woman he loved, is the matter of The Divine War.
Because what else can we do? This is the world we live in. The gods take what they want from us mortals. Our prayers for justice, for protection, for mercy—they fall on deaf ears.
— Eamon (The Divine War, Chapter 1)

Bonds of Love, Fear and Iron

Eamon's world is small and mortal: the woman he shares a cottage with, the friend she lost, the blacksmith whose shop he keeps running, a village that has burned too many pyres. Into that world a god walks in daylight, and everything after is measured against the door he once held shut.

Human Love in a Divine World

Eamon is the mortal heart of The Divine War: Astrid's lover, a man with a scarred face and green eyes who works iron at Rowan's forge and smells of sawdust and leather, who went out into a night that belonged to the gods because the woman he loved had not come home. He got her back. He could not get Natalia back, and he told Astrid so in the cruelest possible terms before he broke and held her and said he was sorry.

He believes the rules because he has watched what happens to those who break them, and he believes the Wild Gods are stories because no divine champion has ever come. He does not stop praying; he only admits he doesn't know why. The chapter's closing promise—that the Wild Gods had heard, and were coming—is aimed as much at Eamon's despair as at Astrid's guilt.

What is public beyond that first chapter is only this: that Astrid accepted a Trickster's blood and became the first Bloodmarked, and that Eamon is the man who stood beside her at Natalia's pyre and stands, in the tale, as the measure of her choice. Whether purely mortal love can hold on to someone who is becoming something else—and whether refusing power is wisdom or only another kind of helplessness—are the questions his story asks of a war he never wanted.

Witness the Test of Human Love

Meet Eamon behind the locked door of the first chapter of The Divine War. His story explores the courage to remain human, the strength found in vulnerability, and the price of the choices that gods offer mortals.
