The River Knew Her Name

The river knew her name, though she had never told it. Khali lives in a small cabin that crouches like a secret between the willows, on a bend of river near the Kingdom of Moradim, tending raised beds of herbs that no village wise woman would grow: dreamroot that grows too eager, moonbell with silver-veined leaves, night jasmine, silver sage—older cultivars that thrive on whispered words and careful intention, that bloom best under starlight and turn bitter if harvested in anger. Smoke drifts from her chimney in spirals that break apart before they can form patterns, one small rebellion against the ordered world beyond her garden gate. She wipes her hands on a skirt that has seen better decades, and her laugh is like water over stones.

Those who find her door have usually exhausted every other option. In the village they say the witch by the river can heal nightmares with song and herb, and the stories grow in the telling—by next season, she suspects, she will have turned into a crone with iron teeth who steals children in the night. What walks among them is something else entirely. In the realm of dreams she is not diminished or hidden: she is exactly what she was always meant to be, a guide through the dark places, a voice singing lost souls back to safety. Her voice carries a soft cadence that makes mortals feel strangely at ease, and something ancient and compassionate flickers in her pale green eyes—understanding gathered across centuries rather than years.

She once wove grand workings, when the world was younger and less careful about its boundaries. Now she grinds herbs for a widow whose seven-year-old dreams of drowning, accepts a few worn coppers with the gravity she might show a king's ransom, and promises to sing by the water when the moon rises full. It is such a small magic. Perhaps that is the point. The tale the Historian Tanneus G'Raja recovered—The Song of Blood and Stone—is the story of what came of that choice, and of how her memory was carved into stone by hands that lied.

Dreams can be many things. Some are gifts. Others are burdens. What kind troubles you?
— Khali to Sophia, the widow at her gate (The Song of Blood and Stone, Chapter 1)

The Heart That Heals in Small Ways

Khali is divine compassion worn lightly. She listens—really listens, as Jade will later say, not just waiting for her turn to speak—and explains things without making anyone feel foolish for not already knowing them. She reads grief in the set of a mother's shoulders and fear in the lines around her eyes, and she has learned that poverty makes every gift a sacrifice. When she is truly seen she is at first alarmed, then quietly grateful; when a pampered princess talks over her, she is charmed despite herself. Beneath the patience is a loneliness she rarely admits: she has been many places, "stayed long enough to help, not long enough to put down roots."

Gently Attentive
Studies each visitor with quiet care, noting how sorrow has aged a young mother "beyond her years," and answers fear with plain, warm honesty rather than mystery.
Anciently Kind
Believes every soul deserves a gentle voice to guide it home in the hours between midnight and dawn—not because mortals worship her, but because they are afraid.
Carefully Hidden
Pulls her humanity around her "like a cloak," deflecting questions about where she came from. "I'm someone who helps with nightmares. That's all that matters right now."
Quietly Lonely
Startled to be offered friendship freely, by someone who does not know what she is. She thinks of brighter beings "who scorched the earth with their glory and never looked back," and stays away from them.
Perhaps the greatest power lay not in mighty demonstrations but in small acts of compassion, in being present when fear threatened to drown hope entirely. Perhaps this was why she had chosen this form, this place, this quiet work among mortals who would never truly know what walked among them.
— The Song of Blood and Stone, Chapter 1

Mistress of the Threshold

Khali's power lives in the liminal space between meaning and music, waking and sleeping—the boundary that has always been thin at her bend of the river, where a thrush sings three notes that almost form a word. She works it through seemingly humble means: herbs, tea, and lullabies. But the lullaby she sings for a frightened princess is ancient, older than human language, "a bridge between waking and sleeping, a path that only those with divine heritage knew how to walk."

Shepherd of Dreams
Dream-Walking: Sings a sleeper down into their dream and follows to observe what their mind is showing them
Prophecy Recognized: Knows the difference between an anxiety dream and true vision, "sharp with the clarity that only prophetic sight could achieve"
Walls Between Minds: Herbs from liminal places, placed beneath a pillow, that muffle the Sight without removing it
Guiding Song: Lullabies "that can guide people to sweeter dreams," sung by the riverbank under the full moon
Healer of Nightmares
Herb-Lore: Dreamroot for gentleness, moonbell to quiet an overactive mind, silver sage against unwanted visions; valerian, passionflower and chamomile for a princess's rest
Careful Doses: "Half a cup when the sun touches the horizon, no more"
Counsel: Understands that a child's dream of drowning is a loss too large for his waking thoughts to hold
Payment: "Whatever you can spare without hardship to yourself or the boy"
Hidden Divinity
Divine Perception: Something in a princess's nightmares "tickled at the edges of her divine perception"; she can feel a faint shimmer of untrained power in another
Old Starlight: The void-thing in the dream names her by taste—"old starlight. Divine marrow"—and its touch begins to unravel her mortal form
Grand Workings: Once wove far greater magic, "when the world was younger and less careful about its boundaries"
Contained Voice: Her singing carries a quality "that marked her divine nature even when carefully contained"
What the River Remembers
River-Sense: Feels the current's pulse "like a second heartbeat," and the memories it carries—joy and sorrow, violence and loss
Older Cultivars: Grows plants that answer to intention and starlight rather than mere soil
Bird-Speech: Hears words in the thrush's song that only she can hear—of threads being woven, of a storm gathering beyond the horizon
Chosen Ground: Picked this place precisely because the boundary between meaning and music has always been thin there

A Goddess by the River

The two chapters of her tale that are online show the quiet work and the first crack in it: a widow's son who dreams of drowning, and a princess of Moradim whose nightmares turn out to be prophecy. What follows—the friendship, the prince from across the ocean, the road to High Pass—is the matter of the rest of The Song of Blood and Stone, and of the Historian's bitter note that prefaces it.

Threads Drawing Tight
A friendship, a foreign prince, and a journey to High Pass. How the river's song ends is told in the tale—and lied about in stone.
The Mural at High Pass
In the stone sanctuary near what was once High Pass, the Historian found a mural: a radiant Vaelari wielding a blade of pure light, striking down a shadowed figure who glowed with ethereal darkness, while a human figure reached toward the fallen god. The standard tale—Light conquering Dark. His own investigation persuaded him otherwise: that "the 'corrupted Vaelari' and the 'dark witch' were simply two people who had loved each other," and that "the stones we carve our stories into are no more truthful than the hands that wield the chisel."
You've seen a possible future, one of many. Between now and then, a thousand choices will be made, a million paths taken. The future is never fixed until it becomes the past.
— Khali to Jade, after the vision (The Song of Blood and Stone, Chapter 2)

Bonds by the Water

Khali's relationships are those of a goddess who has chosen to be needed rather than worshipped—patients who leave with lighter steps, a princess who declares herself a friend "whether you like it or not," and, in the chapters yet to be published, a prince whose blood recognizes something in her voice. The Historian who found her story never met her; he came to know her through Captain Morris's journal and a magistrate's sealed files, and he wrote of her with something close to grief.

Darkness That Was Gentle

Khali, the Dream Shepherd, is a goddess of the threshold—of sleep and dreaming—who chose to walk among mortals as a simple healer. Her cabin by the river near Moradim is a place of comfortable shadows and warm candlelight, of jars carefully labeled and herbs hanging from the rafters like sleeping bats; everything in it speaks of quiet competence, of knowledge gained through practice rather than study. To the village she is the witch by the river. To the frightened people who find her gate she is the last option that turns out to be the right one.

Her story, as it survives online, opens with the smallest of magics: a mother's few coppers, a boy named Tam, a promise to sing at the full moon so that "the water will remember different things for a while." Then a princess sweeps in with unsolicited advice about window boxes and nightmares she cannot take seriously, and Khali discovers that the dreams are true—a vision of Moradim centuries hence, emptied by a hungry silence. She gives Jade what protection she can, and lets the future worry about itself.

What the anthology's blurb tells us, and the Historian's note confirms, is how the tale is remembered afterward: as a mural at High Pass in which a radiant Vaelari strikes down a shadowed witch and balance is restored through sacrifice. Tanneus G'Raja spent years accepting that version. Then he found a captain's private journal and a magistrate's purchased report, and understood that the goddess who spent her existence making the darkness gentle had been rewritten as the darkness itself.

Her own teaching, offered at a royal breakfast table, is the quiet heart of the tale: darkness is not corruption. Night is not the absence of day but its partner. We sleep in darkness, dream in darkness, grow in the darkness of the womb. To fear the dark is to fear half of existence. That the world chose to fear it anyway—and to name her as the reason—is the wound the Historian could not stop pressing.

Enter the Realm of Dreams and Nightmares

Meet the healer by the river in the two chapters that are online, and read the Historian's note on the mural at High Pass. Her story explores the cost of love when gods walk among mortals, and how truth can be corrupted by those who control the narrative.
