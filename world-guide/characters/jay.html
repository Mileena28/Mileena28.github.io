Hope Ignited Through Explosive Purpose

Two figures duck out of the tunnel into the torchlight, "silhouetted against the forest's faint glow," faces smudged with soot, sweat shining. Meridia's shoulders loosen: "Ah! Dominic! Jay! You are returned, and safely, praise the Wild Gods!" Dominic grins. Jay drags a sleeve across his face and nods, eager: "The bombs… they worked great."

He is the younger half of the sanctuary's mission pair — the one who does the binding while Dominic does the shouting. It is Jay who first works out what the white-robed stranger means: "Oh, man… That mob… they were after you, weren't they?" — with a nervous laugh at the thought that he and Dominic had been "runnin' for our lives, thinkin' Zenith's Chosen were huntin' us down for torchin' their precious Hall."

The next morning he is grim. He clears a path through the Forsaken for the hooded captive, "shoving aside anyone who hesitated too long, his jaw set, gaze cold, merciless"; drags a rickety chair forward; produces "rough, frayed rope from somewhere within his scavenged garments" and ties wrists and ankles "in quick, brutal knots." He carries what they took off the man — a travel-worn pack and an old longsword with a faded gold sun on the pommel — and props the blade against the wall with a shrug: "Lifted off some poor refugee, I'd wager." When Dominic's fists start, it is Jay who sets the sagging captive upright "the way a man rights a fence post," and holds him there for the next one.

The bombs… they worked great.
— Jay reporting to Meridia (The Forsaken, Chapter 23)
Story in Progress
Only Chapters 23–24 of The Forsaken are public, and this profile stops there. Jay's missions with Dominic continue in that book, and not all of them end with soot and a grin.

Energy That Refuses to Be Extinguished

Jay says little and does much. The public chapters give him half a dozen lines and a great deal of rope.

Eager
"Jay nodded eagerly, dragging a sleeve across his face." Fresh from setting fire to Zenith's temple, he reports like a boy who has pulled off a dare (Ch. 23).
Hands That Know Knots
Rope from his own garments; wrists and ankles bound to a chair in "quick, brutal knots"; the chair hauled upright again when Dominic throws the man back into it (Ch. 24).
Quick to Connect
He is the first in the cavern to link the mob at Mira's heels to their own flight from the burning Hall — and to laugh, nervously, at the irony (Ch. 23).
Second Pair of Hands
Everything he does in Chapter 24 is in service of Dominic's work: clearing, binding, holding, righting. He does not speak against it.
Oh, man. That mob… they were after you, weren't they? And here we were, runnin' for our lives, thinkin' Zenith's Chosen were huntin' us down for torchin' their precious Hall.
— Jay, on learning who Mira is (The Forsaken, Chapter 23)

Fire and Precision in Service of Freedom

Jay's toolkit, as the public chapters show it: bombs that work, rope that holds, and the nerve to go into Lucien's town twice in two days.

The Fire of Purpose
Jay is the Forsaken's fight at its simplest: a young man who was cast out and found a use for his hands. The bombs work; the rope holds; the report is eager. What the public chapters ask, quietly, is what those hands are willing to do — and Chapter 24 answers by having him right a beaten man like a fence post for the next blow, without a word.
Had this on him, if ya can believe it. Pretty piece, too. Old make. Lifted off some poor refugee, I'd wager.
— Jay, of the captive's sun-pommelled longsword (The Forsaken, Chapter 24)

The Spark of Hope in Resistance

Jay is the smaller shadow beside Dominic — the young man who set the bombs, tied the knots, and worked out before anyone else that the girl in white had been running from the same mob they were. He has fewer lines than any named Forsaken in the public chapters and is present for every important thing that happens in them.

Chapter 23 gives him eagerness and a nervous laugh; Chapter 24 gives him a set jaw and rope. He does not rage as Dominic rages or calculate as Meridia calculates. He clears the way, binds the man, props the old sword against the wall with a shrug about refugees, and, when the captive sags under the fists, sets him upright for more. The book does not comment. It does not need to.

His story continues in The Forsaken, on the road with Dominic. Where it takes him — and what he brings back — is for that book to say.
