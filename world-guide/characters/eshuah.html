A Prince Who Stares East

Eshuah arrives in Moradim from Fayespire, the eastern islands across the ocean, to marry a princess he has never met. Khali meets him at a royal breakfast and her divine senses prickle with recognition: the Vaelari are "descendants of something ancient, a mixing of bloodlines that should never have been possible," and this one carries it in every line of his form—tall and imposing even while seated, with pale blue eyes and black hair that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it. Where Jade is all motion and energy, he is stillness itself, "watching everything with the careful attention of someone far from home." He answers direct questions in minimal words. He spends half his time on the balcony looking east, "like he's trying to see all the way home."

His grandmother told him stories of beings who sang the world into shape, whose voices could still be heard "in the space between sleeping and waking," and taught him that "power leaves marks, like ink staining fingers." So when a hedge-witch sings a lullaby at his betrothed's table—something older than the twilight prayers of his islands—he leans forward as if catching something familiar. He carves driftwood, a habit from home. He asks what happens to raindrops after they fall. He speaks, as Jade complains, in riddles: "The blue remembers the sky. The silver has forgotten what it reflects."

He is bound by duty in the plainest terms: Fayespire needs Moradim's wealth, its trade routes, its connections to the mainland, and without this marriage his islands "will wither like flowers cut from their roots." The Historian's note tells us how the tale is remembered afterward—as a mural at High Pass in which a radiant Vaelari strikes down a shadowed figure, and as an official story of a prince "corrupted" by dark magic. Tanneus G'Raja came to believe that both the Vaelari and the witch in that mural "were simply two people who had loved each other."

Your song. It reminds me of home. Of the twilight prayers. But different. Older.
— Eshuah to Khali, at the royal breakfast (The Song of Blood and Stone)

A Mind Like Deep Water

Eshuah thinks in tides and starlight. His mind, as Khali comes to see it, is "like deep water—still on the surface but with currents that ran to unexpected depths"; he parses layers of meaning in simple words and finds poetry in the prosaic. In company he is unfailingly polite, occasionally warm, and almost always homesick. In the islands, he says, dreams are conversations with the eternal, and forgetting may be the true weight we carry: "Not all lost things are sad. Some are so beautiful that forgetting them is the tragedy."

Watchful Stillness
Says almost nothing at a tense royal breakfast and studies everyone with "those unsettling blue eyes." His homesickness sits on him like a physical thing.
Poet, Not Diplomat
"Sometimes talking to you is like solving riddles," his betrothed sighs. Khali translates: the blue silk has a character of its own; the silver only shows what surrounds it.
Bound by Duty
"A political alliance that will save my people from starvation." His parents' marriage was arranged; thirty years in the same palace, and he never saw them touch without an audience.
Blood That Recognizes
"Power recognizes power," his grandmother said. "The divine can't hide from eyes that carry even a drop of eternal blood." He sees shadows bend "slightly wrong" around the healer, and asks what she really is.
Sometimes I feel as though I'm in the presence of something vast and ancient wearing the disguise of someone I could love.
— Eshuah to Khali, by the riverside (The Song of Blood and Stone)

Gifts of the Eastern Islands

Eshuah's abilities are those of his blood and his upbringing: the Vaelari inheritance that lets him feel a goddess in a lullaby, and the education of a prince who must understand trade routes because thousands of lives depend on them. He carries a sword and practices his forms at dawn. He carries a knife and carves memories out of driftwood. Neither is what makes him dangerous to a jealous court; that is simply the way he looks at someone who understands him.

From Fayespire to High Pass

Eshuah does not appear in the two chapters that are online; he arrives in the third, "today from Fayespire," at a breakfast where the queen calls Khali a witch and Khali calls darkness the partner of day. What grows between the prince and the healer over the following weeks—translated, at first, by his own betrothed—is the tale's slow heart, and its ending is the subject of the Historian's note.

The Vaelari
In the Historian's notes the Vaelari are celestial-elven hybrids—"born when celestial desire meets elven grace," beings who "drift through mist-veiled shores like walking poetry, beautiful and terrible in their otherworldly power." Their islands seem to answer to their moods, volcanic activity rising with collective distress and calming with peace. In Eshuah's day those islands were Fayespire; the world knows them now as Sommer Isle. What happened there in the Divine War to change the name is another tale.
The blue remembers the sky. The silver has forgotten what it reflects.
— Eshuah, choosing silk for the wedding (The Song of Blood and Stone)

Bonds of Duty and Recognition

Eshuah's bonds are few and heavy: a people whose survival hangs on his marriage, a betrothed who tries so hard to understand him, and a healer who finishes his thoughts before he has fully formed them. "You're good for him, Khali," Jade says, early on. "Good for both of us."

The Radiant Vaelari of the Mural

Prince Eshuah of Fayespire is the displaced heir of the anthology's title card—a Vaelari, celestial-elven, brought across the Azure Expanse to secure by marriage the trade his volcanic islands cannot live without. He is contemplative to the point of silence, poetic to the point of exasperating his betrothed, and homesick in every line of his body. He carves driftwood into memories, asks strange questions about raindrops, and hears in a hedge-witch's lullaby something older than the twilight prayers of home.

His grandmother taught him that the divine can't hide from eyes that carry even a drop of eternal blood, and the tale is in large part the story of what he sees when he looks at Khali—and what he chooses to do about a heart that "leans toward someone it has no right to want" while a kingdom's survival hangs on his duty. Those chapters are not yet online.

What is public is the ending's afterlife. In the sanctuary near what was once High Pass a mural shows a radiant Vaelari wielding a blade of pure light against a shadowed figure; the official story calls him corrupted and calls his death a sacrifice against the dark. The Historian who spent years revering that mural came to read it as "a monument to mortal lies," and to write of the prince and the healer as two people who had loved each other. Fayespire itself would become Sommer Isle before the age was out—but that is the Divine War's story, not his.

Experience the Tragedy of Recognition

Begin with the healer by the river, whose song the prince will one day recognize. His story explores the cost of carrying divine heritage in a mortal world, and the beauty of finding true connection in impossible circumstances.
