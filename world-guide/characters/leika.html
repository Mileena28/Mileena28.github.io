Granite Given Breath

Before the world learned to name itself, when stars were still settling into their first dance, there walked the First—the architect-gods who had sung substance from the void and given form to the formless. Leika raised the mountains from her breath, teaching stone to reach skyward. She is vast as continents and patient as geological time, and when she rises "from the celestial foundations like granite given breath and terrible purpose," stone dust falls from her shoulders in glittering cascades and the very floor of the divine realm cracks and reforms beneath her, "unable to contain the weight of one who had lifted mountain ranges from the sea."

Her form is magnificent and terrible—part woman, part living landscape. Crystalline formations jut from her skin like armor forged from the earth's bones. Her hair flows like lava, slow and unstoppable. Her eyes hold the deep green of ancient forests, the patient brown of fertile soil, and the stark white of eternal peaks. Each word she speaks carries the weight of mountains behind it, sending ripples through the divine space; when she shrugs, somewhere far below, a mountain trembles.

Her judgment of her youngest brother's creation is the first spoken in The God Who Wept, and the plainest. She holds up the delicate skeleton of a human child—"bones like bird wings, hollow and light"—and closes her fist, and the bones crumble to dust. "My nymphs and dryads dance with the wind and sleep in the earth—they know their place in the eternal cycle... They are permanent as the mountains they tend, enduring as the stones they guard. But these creatures of yours? They bloom like flowers in a single season." Ages later, in the age after the Great Withdrawal, the nature spirits are still walking the wild places—"Leika's children, if the oldest texts are to be believed"—and scholars still ask what the Mother of Mountains had in mind when she breathed life into stone and stream.

Stone endures, Thainos. Clay crumbles.
— Leika, on the making of humanity (The God Who Wept, Chapter 1)

The Immutable Authority

Leika thinks in the long view of stone. She measures worth by endurance and finds the brief, breakable, self-destroying creatures of clay wanting—not from petty malice, the tale is careful to say, but "from the terrible wisdom of those who had shaped existence itself." Thainos's answer to her—"Your mountains were once volcanic ash and settling sediment, sister. Should I have declared them worthless before they found their strength?"—does not move her. Mountains endure because they accept their nature.

Geological Patience
"Vast as continents, patient as geological time." She judges in epochs, and expects to be proven right by them.
Contempt for the Fragile
"Look at your little figures, Thainos. So breakable. So... fragile." She demonstrates with a child's skeleton and a closed fist.
Certain of the Cycle
Her nymphs and dryads know their place in the eternal cycle and their purpose in the grand design. Clay, she is sure, will "tear each other apart" by tomorrow.
Unmoved by Eloquence
To her brother's most impassioned speech she answers, in a voice like distant earthquakes, that his mortals "will break themselves trying to be what they can never become."
Mountains endure because they accept their nature. Your mortals will break themselves trying to be what they can never become.
— Leika to Thainos (The God Who Wept, Chapter 1)

Shaper of Worlds

Leika's power is the power of the earth's bones: she raised mountain ranges from oceanic depths, and reality itself struggles to bear her weight. Her creations tend the wild places she made, and long after the gods fell silent, those creations remain—one of the few threads of the divine still active in the world, and the subject of a young elf's research journal in Voices of the Wild.

From Creation to the Silent Age

Leika's story runs from the first breath of the world to its silent aftermath. She sits with the First in the Hall of Eternal Judgment, the crystalline amphitheater where the gods sit in judgment. What was argued before that Hall in The Divine War, and how the First Gods answered, is the anthology's final tale.

The First Judgment
Between her fingers she materialized the delicate skeleton of a human child—"bones like bird wings, hollow and light and utterly defensible against the forces of a harsh world"—and with deliberate care she closed her fist. The bones crumbled to dust. It was not cruelty as she understood it. It was a demonstration: what is made of clay does not last, and what does not last, in the Mother of Mountains' reckoning, cannot matter.

Foundations and Fractures

Leika's bonds are the bonds of the First: siblings who each shaped a portion of creation and judged the others' work, and a youngest brother who chose flesh. Below her, in the world she helped make, her spirits tend the mountains and forests, and mortals of a later age still wonder what she meant by them.

The Mountain That Does Not Bend

Leika, Mother of Mountains, Shaper of Worlds, is the first of the First Gods to speak in The God Who Wept, and the one whose verdict on humanity is carved most simply: stone endures, clay crumbles. She raised the mountains from her breath and lifted ranges from the sea; she is part woman and part living landscape, with lava for hair and armor of crystal, and where she walks the floor of heaven cracks. Her nymphs and dryads know their place in the eternal cycle. Her brother's humans, she is certain, will bloom for a season and tear each other apart.

She is not a villain in the tale's telling. The First Gods "had spoken not from petty malice but from the terrible wisdom of those who had shaped existence itself"—and the Historian's own final testament, written at the end of the anthology, concedes that the distant, hierarchical gods he spent his life condemning may have been right about the danger of loving mortals too much. Whether Leika's stone-hearted judgment was cruelty or foresight is the argument the Divine War is fought over.

Her legacy in the mortal world outlasts the age of the gods. After the Great Withdrawal, when the Wild Gods retreated into hidden places and prayers began echoing unanswered into the void, the nature spirits—Leika's children—were still walking the wild places. What the Mother of Mountains had in mind when she breathed life into stone and stream is a question Voices of the Wild takes up long after she herself has fallen silent.
