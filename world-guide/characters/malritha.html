Born from Emptiness

Malritha was born during the deepest moment of lunar eclipse, when the moon hung like an open wound in the night sky. The women of her line—seers all, guardians of the ancient moon temples—had gathered to welcome another daughter blessed by celestial grace, burning sacred oils in silver vessels and chanting hymns that had not changed in ten thousand years. Yet when the child emerged, no cry pierced the silence. Frost crept across the stone floor from her first exhalation. Far below the temple the tide retreated, exposing seafloor that had never known air, and somewhere in the deep something vast and cold stirred, sensing a kindred emptiness born into the world.

The midwife saw it first: something ancient looking back through newborn eyes—not evil, but emptiness, a void where wonder should have bloomed. The eldest seer, blood tears tracking down her ancient face, named the child. "She is born of the veiled moon, and her path will be shrouded. I have seen her in dreams that taste of salt and iron. She will bring great power and greater suffering. Both hers. Both ours."

Before Elodias and Senia were bound by their curse, there was Malritha—the lunar seer whose jealous heart wove fate itself into a weapon. The Historian Tanneus G'Raja found her account sealed in a blackwood box in a sea cave beneath the cliffs of old Quenos, its script mirrored, illegible by torchlight and revealed only beneath the rising moon. "This is not a tale you'll hear sung," he warns. "Not in the markets of Atheria, nor in the sanctified halls that now bless its name. But the wind remembers. The sea remembers. And some griefs are woven too deeply to ever stay buried."

Everything. Nothing. The paths between.
— Malritha, aged twelve, asked by the temple matriarch what she sees in the threads (The Weaver of Sorrows, Chapter VI)

The Hollow Mind

Malritha grew like shadow stretching across stone—inevitable, silent, somehow wrong in a way no one could name. Where other children of the temple ran with graceful limbs and laughter, she moved with unhurried deliberation, her footfalls making no sound. The acolytes whispered that she did not sleep, that her bed was always cold, that she had asked what it feels like to dream "as if she doesn't know," that she watched people "like she's studying how to be human." Even her own mother grew distant beneath the unblinking gaze of a child who never sought comfort, never reached for an embrace, never shed tears when others would have broken.

Hollow
A void where wonder should have bloomed. Fevers left her skin burning while her eyes stayed cold and calculating; by her twentieth year some claimed they could see through her in certain lights, as if she were becoming translucent.
Uncannily Gifted
Her visions were too precise to ignore: the king in the north dead tomorrow with a fishbone in his throat, the three-day storm and the exact lightning that would split the harbormaster's door, the mountain passes closing with unexpected snow, twin princes and which would live to see sunrise.
Never Visited
Other oracles fell into trances, their mouths moving with words not their own. Malritha knew only silence. "She was merely the conduit, never the chosen. The vessel, never the visited."
Alone in the Pattern
She could see the tapestry of every other life, but "her own remained a barren thread, singular and cold, untouched by the warmth of connection, unbranched by possibility"—until he came.
Content Warning
The Weaver of Sorrows is the darkest account in The Lost Histories. Its later chapters contain obsession, stalking and voyeurism, sexual content and psychological manipulation. Malritha's origins evoke sympathy; what she chooses to become does not.

Gifts of the Eclipse

She found her purpose in the weaving halls, amid the ancient looms where seers had woven prophecy into cloth for generations—but ordinary thread held no allure for her. The elders found her one night standing before the bleached ribs of a leviathan that had beached itself the very night of her birth and lain three days on the shore untouched by any scavenger. Within a fortnight she had made of those bones something terrible and magnificent.

From Vessel to Weaver of Sorrows

The star-readers of the Almanac tell it in a breath: born hollow during a lunar eclipse, Malritha loved Elodias with the terrible hunger of one who had never felt before, and when he sought her help finding Senia, she wove threads of shadow and blood into reality itself. The Weaver of Sorrows tells it slowly, and it is not a kind telling.

Bonds That Bind and Break

Malritha's connections are few, and every one of them is a wound: a temple that valued her visions and feared her presence, a god who came to her for help, and a goddess whose face she would come to know better than her own.

The Empty Heart That Broke the World

Malritha is the shadow side of the tale of Elodias and Senia—the seer whose jealous heart wove fate itself into a weapon. Born during a lunar eclipse to a line of moon-temple seers, she came into the world without a cry, with frost on the floor and the tide fleeing the shore, and with something ancient looking out through newborn eyes. She grew angular and silent, sleepless and unembraced, and found her only company in a loom she built from the bones of a leviathan and threaded with moonlight pulled from the sky.

Her gift was real. Her visions were too precise to ignore, and her loomwork, the Historian says, rivalled the earliest seers. But no god ever spoke to her as they spoke to the other oracles; she was the conduit, never the chosen; the vessel, never the visited. Her own thread stayed barren and cold in the pattern of the world—until a god who walked as a man came to her for help, and a hollow heart felt for the first time.

What she did with that feeling made her the Weaver of Sorrows. The Almanac says she wove threads of shadow and blood into reality itself and cursed the lovers; the coast of old Quenos still bears the mark; and the name Malritha, the Historian notes, appears nowhere in temple records—no cult claims her, no shrine bears her mark. What remains is a mirrored scroll in a sea cave, a skeleton with silver thread at its wrist, and a warning: obsession is not devotion.
