Guardians of Older Truths

The Historian's compendium describes the Free People as nomadic tribes living outside established authority, maintaining older spiritual traditions and a settled resistance to institutional control — people who "possess advanced techniques for recognizing divine nature and preserving dangerous truths." They keep religious practices that predate the temple societies: communion with plant and animal spirits, reverence for ancestors, star reading, dream sharing, and the care of sites of supernatural significance. Their councils are circles in which every adult voice carries equal weight; their elders are wisdom keepers; their path walkers know the safe routes through supernatural territory.

In The God Who Wept, Thainos meets one such band far to the east, beyond the reach of Governor Verin's authority, in a valley where the aftermath of war has left abandoned homesteads and silted irrigation channels. He finds their shrine first — river stones stacked with reverent care, an arch of woven branches, feathers and smooth pebbles and bundles of herbs laid at the charred roots of an enormous oak that lightning has split open and failed to kill. "It was grief and hope, ruin and reverence, all bound together in a single, silent testimony." Then a voice behind him: "You should not be here, stranger. This place is not for your kind."

The speaker is Marra, a woman of middle years, "her face lined with hardship and resilience, her dark hair streaked heavily with grey," standing at the centre of five men and three women with unstrung bows and spears — not raised, but ready. Her gaze is "sharp, searching, missing nothing," and she can smell "settled authority" on his clothes. When he tells them, with a dry smile, that he is no authority — "Quite the opposite" — and that he and the Governor's jurisdiction have parted over "certain… philosophical differences, let's say," her suspicion softens into curiosity, and something like kinship in shared defiance. "Names have power, stranger," she tells him. "We'll call you Stranger, then, until we know more… I am Marra, elder of the Free People. We bow to no distant rulers, nor do we accept their hollow authority over these lands. These woods remember older truths."

I am Marra, elder of the Free People. We bow to no distant rulers, nor do we accept their hollow authority over these lands. These woods remember older truths.
— Marra, at the Lightning Oak
A Note on This Page
Only the first chapter of The God Who Wept is online; Marra and her people belong to a later one. This page describes who they are and how they receive the Stranger. What they see in him by their fire, and what comes of it, is the account's to tell.

Community of the Unbowed

Marra's band lives in a hidden valley sheltered by pine and alder — "practical, temporary structures woven from branches and stretched hides, arranged in a loose circle around a central fire." She leads it not by decree but by a word that settles over the group "like a quiet decree" all the same: "He will share our fire tonight, and his story. Then, we will decide."

Fierce Independence
"We bow to no distant rulers." Marra to an officer of the settled powers: "We are not 'tribes,' soldier. We are the Free People."
Wary Hospitality
"Night falls fast in these hills. Our camp is close. You can share our fire. In the morning, we'll decide whether you move on — or stay." A rough wooden cup of fermented drink; roasted game, wild roots, preserved berries.
Sharp Judgment
"The settled powers — those who build walls and draw borders — always fear what they cannot neatly classify, what they cannot easily control." She hears the practice in the Stranger's explanations, and says so.
Plain Speech
A one-armed elder, when the Stranger names what he searches for: "Then, friend… you'll be walking a long while yet… No one who's ever drawn breath on this earth fits what you seek."
We are not 'tribes,' soldier. We are the Free People. We bow to no governor's edicts, nor do we acknowledge the legitimacy of laws imposed by distant strangers.
— Marra, to a soldier of the settled powers

Voices of Earth and Ancient Wisdom

The Free People's practices are recorded in the Historian's compendium as well as in the account, and the two agree: theirs is a knowledge kept outside temples and archives, carried in herbs, songs and custom.

Vision-Smoke
What It Does: "reveal truth beneath surface appearances… pierce through divine disguises and reveal the essential nature of beings and objects" (compendium)
Marra's Words: "The smoke shows what words cannot. It reveals not who you claim to be — but who you are beneath the naming. Beneath the forgetting."
The Herbs: plants "known to open the mind, to blur the veil between waking and vision," added to the fire one by one "with reverent precision"; the compendium names truthweed, voidleaf, memorybark, spiritblossom and timesprout
Its Keeping: a bundle of hide "set apart from the rest of the community's belongings"
Governance
Circle Councils: "democratic decision-making bodies where all adult voices carry equal weight"
Wisdom Keepers: "elderly individuals who preserve important knowledge and provide guidance"
Path Walkers: "specialists who know safe routes through supernatural territories"
Truth Speakers and Bridge Builders: those trained to resist compulsion; those who carry word between tribes and settlements
Living With the Land
Moving Through It: "a subtle path invisible to untrained eyes — an instinctual map known only to those who lived and breathed in rhythm with the wild"
Signs of Them: an improvised log bridge; trunks carved with old symbols; beehives hollowed from fallen wood; a carefully buried fire pit
Camp: branches and stretched hides in a loose circle; children learning to twist cord from bark
Sacred Sites: "caring for locations of supernatural significance" — the Lightning Oak among them
Older Traditions
Nature Communion: "direct communication with plant and animal spirits"
Ancestor Reverence: "ongoing relationships with deceased family and tribal members"
Star Reading and Dream Sharing: navigation and prophecy by the sky; "collective exploration of the unconscious realm"
Memory: "living repositories of suppressed knowledge and alternative perspectives" — hidden archives, ways of escape, "truth verification"

The Stranger at the Fire

The shape of the encounter can be given without its ending. A shrine, a challenge, a fire, a story; then the seeing smoke.

The Lightning Oak
Sacred Ground
The Stranger kneels at the shrine beneath the split oak — "not to pray… but simply to acknowledge, in quiet reverence, the sacred impulse that had led to its creation." Marra's band finds him there.
Cautious Welcome
"Most people flee from these lands, not toward them." A stranger at odds with the Governor earns a fire and a night, and a decision in the morning.
His Story
Balance, harmony between people and the earth, cooperation over obedience — "those ideas can be dangerous, in the wrong ears." Around the fire, "the stillness was no longer suspicion — it was recognition."
Marra's Question
"And what is it you seek, Stranger, in all this endless wandering?" His answer — one soul untouched by cruelty — draws a stunned silence, and the one-armed elder's blunt reply.
The Seeing Smoke
He speaks of gods "as though you have met them." Marra does not believe his correction. "Bring the seeing smoke… It is time we learn more of our guest. The path beneath the path."
Beneath the Naming
What the Free People see in the smoke, and what interrupts it, is the substance of the chapter — and of the account's last movement.
Why They Matter
Of all the mortals on the Wanderer's thousand-year road, the Free People are the ones with a rite for seeing what he is. Their vision-smoke, the compendium says, "can pierce through divine disguises" — and Marra orders it lit for the Stranger who speaks of gods as if he has argued with them. That the seeing happens by a shrine of grief and hope, at the roots of a tree that lightning could not kill, is the kind of thing this account does on purpose.
The smoke shows what words cannot. It reveals not who you claim to be—but who you are beneath the naming. Beneath the forgetting.
— Marra, as the seeing smoke rises

Freedom That Cannot Be Classified

Marra and the Free People are the community the Wanderer meets after the settled world has finished with him — nomads in a wild valley who keep a shrine at a wounded tree, share their fire with a stranger on condition of hearing his story, and possess, in the seeing smoke, a rite for learning "who you are beneath the naming." Marra herself is the account's clearest portrait of authority without institution: a grey-streaked woman whose word carries "like a quiet decree" and whose gaze misses nothing, least of all a practised explanation.

The Historian's compendium places them in a wider frame: tribes outside every established authority, keeping traditions older than the temples, serving as "living repositories of suppressed knowledge." What the account adds is a face and a voice — "We bow to no distant rulers" — and a night by a fire in which people who have never been counted by any governor turn out to be the ones equipped to see a god.

Their name is older than Marra's band. It belongs, in The Divine War, to the village of Astrid Ravenscroft in the age when mortals hid from the gods after dark — and what became of those Free People, and of the land they lived on, is that account's to tell. Here, in the east, at the roots of the Lightning Oak, it is enough that they are still free, still watching the roads, and still lighting the smoke for the strangers who come.
