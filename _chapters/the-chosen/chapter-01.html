The sea had always sung to Mira Roehart: a tongue of tides and whispers, salt-laced winds, and the groan of ancient piers. She knew its changes as she knew the sounds of her own home. Even now, leaning against the moss-softened railing, she could hear the water drawing back beneath her feet. But other sounds had gathered over it: a rasping cough, the scrape of a chest dragged across the boards, a woman asking where they were meant to go.

Another boat had come in.

Mira moved aside to let its passengers pass. A Solarian woman gripped the railing with both hands, waiting for the pier to steady beneath her. Behind her, a man carried a bundle against his chest with such care that Mira looked for a child’s face among the folds. There was only cloth, tied at the corners. Neither looked toward the town until they reached the shore.

Five years had passed since the Silence, and still the boats arrived.

Some brought Solarians who had little left to say of the city they had lost. Others carried Atherians from failing fields, their hands cracked by work that could no longer feed them. They came with household goods wrapped in blankets, with tools, with nothing. Mira had watched people step ashore and remain beside their belongings long after the boat was emptied, reluctant to leave the last small heap of things that was theirs.

Beyond them, the sea persisted in its cold, indifferent majesty. The tide clawed at the shore, etching and re-etching the coastline. Wind brought the tang of brine and the promise of fish, and farther along the harbor, men bent over their nets. By evening there would be washing strung between the crowded houses. Someone would find room beside a hearth. Life took root in the crevices of ruin, though Mira was beginning to wonder how much more Rivenglade could hold.

The town’s narrow streets were crowded now. Merchants spread salvaged treasures upon rough cloths, offering silverwork beside chipped bowls and garments folded to conceal their wear. Voices rose over one another in the market. From the workshops came the clang of hammers, continuing well beyond the hour when the craftsmen had once put down their tools. Every inn was full.

From the water, it might have looked like prosperity.

Mira had seen a woman turn a ring around her finger for most of a morning before drawing it off and laying it among the things for sale. She had watched a buyer examine it, name a price, and wait. The woman had taken what he offered.

At the foot of the pier, the newly arrived passengers hesitated where the street divided. A man pointed them toward the inns. When one of them asked what a bed would cost, he lowered his hand.

Mira tucked a strand of dark hair behind her ear and looked back toward the boat. There were more people waiting to come ashore.

Lucien Altheris came while Rivenglade was learning to make room.

Mira first saw him beyond the pier, where the cobblestones widened before the market. People were turning to watch him pass. His robes were white and gold, their unfamiliar symbols catching the light as the fabric moved around his ankles. Against the weathered houses and the salt-stained clothes of those gathered there, the white was almost painful to look at.

His dark hair was drawn back from a face of unsettling perfection. Yet it was his eyes that held her: an impossible blue, luminous even when he passed into the shade. Mira followed him with her gaze, trying to catch the reflection that made them shine. The light remained.

He stopped where the market opened toward the harbor. Those nearest him fell quiet. Others continued calling their prices until they noticed that no one was listening, and gradually the voices ceased all the way to the pier.

“You have suffered,” he said.

His voice reached Mira clearly. Smooth as polished stone, rich as honey wine, it carried without the strain she heard in the vendors’ cries. She straightened from the railing.

“You have been driven from your homes. You have buried those you loved. And when you asked why, what answer were you given?”

A woman near the end of the pier lowered her eyes. Beside her, a man shifted his bundle from one arm to the other and waited.

“The gods you knew offered you no certainty. You were told to endure their anger, to seek their favor, to accept what you could not understand.” Lucien looked over the gathering. “But you have not been forgotten.”

Mira felt herself lean nearer. Around her, people had begun to move toward the shore, finding places between the market stalls. A chest remained in the middle of the pier where its owner had set it down.

Lucien spoke of Zenith.

One God, he told them. One source of light and order, untouched by the failings of the deities they had feared and worshipped. The Wild Gods were lesser powers; some were deceivers, drawing mortals away from the truth. Zenith alone could lead them out of the wreckage.

Mira glanced toward the sea. A wave broke against the pilings, scattering spray through the gaps beneath her feet. She had heard the old gods named in gratitude when the fishing boats came home, in anger when they did not. She had never heard them dismissed so easily.

“The Silence was a tribulation,” Lucien said. “You have lived through it without knowing its purpose. Now you are called to step into the light.”

He spoke of a cleansing, of the righteous separated from the sinful, the Chosen from the Forsaken. The words reached people who had spent years asking what they had done to deserve their losses. Mira waited for someone to object. Instead, a man near the shore bowed his head, his lips moving as Lucien continued.

There was purpose in it, then. Their suffering had brought them somewhere.

Mira wanted to feel the relief she saw upon his face. For a moment, with that beautiful voice carrying over the water, she nearly could. It would have been a comfort to believe that every boat had been coming toward this shore for a reason; that the woman with the ring had lost nothing Zenith would not restore.

Lucien lifted his hand.

“You need wander no longer.”

The woman beside Mira began to cry. She made little sound, pressing her fingers against her mouth while tears slipped over them. Someone took her other hand. Neither looked away from him.

Mira stayed until he finished. When his voice fell quiet, she could hear the ropes creaking against the mooring posts again. Then people began calling to him from the shore, asking questions, drawing closer. She caught his name as it passed along the pier.

Lucien Altheris. The Son of Zenith.

In the days that followed, his words traveled farther than his voice.

Mira heard them over market counters and through open windows. People who had scarcely spoken except to ask the price of bread lingered together after their purchases, repeating what he had said. The woman who sold her ring returned to the market with another woman beside her. They stopped beneath an awning to talk, and for the first time Mira saw her smile.

That was what made it difficult.

The relief was real. She could see it in the way people greeted one another, in the eagerness with which they gathered whenever Lucien was to speak. Kat came looking for her with news of each gathering, certain that the next would give Mira what the last had failed to.

Buildings were cleared for worship. Pale cloth appeared in doorways, and the craftsmen began working Zenithian symbols into their wares: spare lines and angles that promised light and order. Prayers drifted down streets where neighbors had once called across their thresholds. The harbor wind caught the words and carried them out over the boats.

Mira still went to the pier. From there she could watch Rivenglade’s windows kindle at dusk and listen to the water beneath the boards. Sometimes the prayers rose loudly enough to cover it.

She had grown up with other stories. Woodland spirits whispered of beside the hearth; sea deities named over nets; ancient powers whose presence belonged to the deep forests and the pines bending beneath the wind. The Wild Gods could be cruel. She knew that. The sea that fed Rivenglade could take its fishermen, and no offering made a boat proof against a storm.

But she had stood among the trees after rain, breathing pine and wet earth, and felt no wish to be delivered from them.

Now she found herself listening before she spoke of such things. The old names brought a patient look to Kat’s face, followed by an explanation Mira had already heard from Lucien. Each time, Kat offered it with the same gentle conviction, as though Mira had only failed to understand.

“Mira?”

She turned from the water. Katalina Petari was coming along the pier, holding her skirts clear of a damp coil of rope. She stepped over it and joined Mira at the railing, close enough that their sleeves touched. For a moment they watched the sinking sun together.

“Did you hear him today?” Kat asked. “I looked for you afterward.”

“I was here.”

“I thought you might have gone home.” Kat’s hazel-green eyes brightened. “Then you heard what he said about the Chosen.”

Mira nodded. Kat waited, smiling, and she searched for an answer that would leave the smile intact.

“He speaks beautifully.”

“He does. But it’s more than that, isn’t it?” Kat rested her hands upon the railing. “All this time, no one could tell us what was going to happen. No one knew what we were supposed to do. Now there’s a way forward.”

Beyond her, the last passengers from the day’s boat were making their way ashore. Mira watched a man pause to look back at the vessel before following the others.

“Do you believe all of it?” she asked.

Kat turned toward her. “Yes.”

“Even what he says about the Wild Gods?”

“They led people away from Zenith.”

“You never used to think that.”

“I didn’t know.” Kat said it without embarrassment. She shifted closer, trying to catch Mira’s eyes. “Neither did you. That’s why he’s here.”

Mira rubbed a patch of damp moss from beneath her thumb. She remembered Kat listening to the same hearthside stories, asking questions long after the others had grown sleepy. She had always wanted to know how to honor what she could not see. Mira had loved that about her.

“What if there’s more than he tells us?” she asked.

“More than Zenith?”

“More than one way to understand it.”

Kat’s smile faded. She looked toward the shore, where people were still gathered beneath the market awnings, then back at Mira.

“After everything that’s happened, don’t you want to be certain of something?”

“I want to, Kat. I truly do.” Mira let the damp moss fall from her fingers. “But he makes it sound as though everything we believed before was wrong.”

“If it was, wouldn’t you want to know?”

Mira had no answer ready. Kat laid a hand over hers upon the railing, warming the chilled fingers.

“There is only one truth,” she said. “Zenith is the source of all light and order. The Silence, the suffering—all of it had a purpose. He was preparing the Chosen for His reign.”

Below them, water surged around a piling and drew away in long strands of foam. Mira watched until the next wave broke them apart.

“I wish you could feel it,” Kat said.

“So do I.”

The sun had thinned to a molten sliver. Across the water, orange deepened into bloodred, and the wet pilings shone beneath them. Mira lingered over the colors as the light slipped away, unwilling to turn toward the shore just yet.

“Will you come with me next time?” Kat asked.

Mira looked at her friend. “Yes. I’ll come.”

Kat smiled and slipped her arm through Mira’s. Together they walked toward the lamps of Rivenglade. From an open doorway came the sound of prayer, quiet at first, then gathering as more voices joined.

Mira turned her head toward the water. The tide was coming in; she could see it lifting the boats against their ropes.

She could no longer hear it.
