The Zenithian Hall pressed in around Mira. Incense gathered beneath the hastily raised rafters, its cloying sweetness thickening as the benches filled. Rough-spun white cloth covered the windows. Sunlight passed through the weave and lay pale across the congregation, bleaching the color from faces turned toward the front.

Beside her, Kat smoothed a fold in her skirt and settled comfortably upon the wooden bench. Mira tried to do the same. The unfamiliar dress pulled across her knees when she shifted, and the fabric cinched at her waist gave little when she drew a breath. She slipped a finger beneath it, then stopped when Kat glanced down.

“Humility,” Kat had said that morning, helping her arrange the dress. “We should show it in how we present ourselves before Zenith.”

Mira had let her straighten the sleeves. She had promised to come, and Kat had looked so pleased when she saw her ready.

At the far end of the hall, Lucien Altheris stood in the light from a covered window. Gold glimmered along his white robes whenever he moved his hands. His voice reached every bench, rich and unhurried, and Mira tried to follow it. For a while she could. Then a phrase would pass without meaning, and by the time she reached for it he had moved on, carrying everyone else with him.

Across the aisle, a woman listened with her eyes closed. Kat leaned forward slightly, her hands still in her lap. Mira looked from one to the other, then back at Lucien. She could hear the beauty of his voice. She could not find what they found inside it.

A breeze lifted the edge of the cloth at the nearest window. Beyond it, a branch stirred in the sunlight.

Her thoughts followed it into the forest. She imagined light spilling through the canopy, scattered coins of gold upon the moss; the yielding earth beneath her bare feet; the smell of pine and loam and leaves softening into soil. There, the air would be cool enough to draw deeply into her lungs. She could sit beneath a tree until the damp came through her clothes, and no one would ask her to move.

Kat’s elbow caught her in the ribs.

Mira straightened. Her friend glanced pointedly at the parchment in Mira’s lap and silently formed the words, Take notes.

She picked up the charcoal. Lucien was speaking of the life awaiting the Chosen beneath Zenith’s reign, but she had missed too much to know where to begin. She made a small mark near the edge of the parchment, curved it inward, and followed it with another. Soon a coil of dark spirals spread beneath her hand.

A strand of hair slipped across her brow. She blew upward to lift it. It rose briefly, then fell back into the same place. Beside her, Kat gave a small shake of her head without looking away from Lucien.

When the sermon ended, Mira was still holding the charcoal over the unfinished spiral. Around her, people drew breath and began to move. Benches scraped against the floor; folded hands opened to touch a neighbor’s arm. The hall filled with conversation, and she could feel the relief of those beside her before she understood that theirs was different from her own.

Kat turned to her, smiling. “Come. There are people I’d like you to meet.”

Mira folded the parchment over its blackened surface and rose.

They made their way between the benches. Kat stopped to greet someone, then someone else, finding an answer for every welcome. Mira stood beside her and waited for a place in the conversation. Once, she thought she had found one, but another woman began speaking at the same moment. Mira closed her mouth. By the time the woman finished, what she had meant to say no longer belonged.

Someone farther along called Kat’s name. She touched Mira’s arm before stepping away.

“Stay a little while. I’ll come back.”

Mira nodded and moved out of the passage between the benches. Near the window, the incense was less oppressive. She watched Kat join a group of women, her face opening in recognition as one of them took her hand.

“Mira, dear.”

A woman in white linen had stopped beside her. Gold filigree edged the woman’s sleeves, fine enough that Mira could distinguish the individual leaves as she lifted a hand toward Mira’s dress.

“How lovely to see you wearing something like this.”

Mira looked down. “Thank you.”

“You should do so more often. It suits you much better than those things you wear into the woods.” The woman paused, her gaze passing over the skirt. “Though you might want another layer beneath it. The cloth is rather thin against the light.”

Mira stepped away from the window.

“I didn’t realize.”

“No harm done. You’ll know for next time.”

She smiled, and Mira tried to return it. Kat had helped her dress. She had stood still for the sleeves, the waist, the careful settling of the folds, and still there was something she ought to have noticed.

“My other clothes are easier to walk in,” she said.

“For the woods, certainly. But we must make a little effort here.”

“I did.”

The answer came more quickly than she intended. The woman’s smile held, though her eyes settled upon Mira’s face.

“Of course, dear. And a little water would take care of that.”

Mira lifted a hand to her cheek. “What?”

“There. A little Rivenglade soil.” The woman indicated the place upon her own face. “We bring enough of the world’s troubles through these doors. We needn’t bring its dirt as well.”

Heat rose beneath Mira’s fingers. She rubbed at the mark, unable to tell whether it had gone.

“Purity in small things,” the woman added. “How can Zenith’s light shine through tarnished glass?”

She patted Mira’s arm and returned to the women near the aisle. One made room for her, lifting a sleeve clear of the bench. A moment later they laughed at something Mira could not hear.

She lowered her hand. There was a faint smear upon her fingertips. She rubbed it against her thumb until it disappeared, then looked for Kat.

Her friend was listening to a woman who held both her hands while she spoke. Kat nodded, her expression intent and kind. Mira knew that look. It had accompanied scraped knees, foolish plans, evenings when she had said too much and wished she could take the words back. Kat had always made room for her.

She could go over. She could wait beside them until they finished, explain what had happened, and let Kat tell her that she ought not to mind. But then she would have to repeat the woman’s words. She would have to hear herself explain why a little dirt and a thin dress had been enough to make her want to leave.

Mira looked around the hall. People were drawing close to hear one another, making plans, reaching across benches to keep a conversation from ending. She wanted someone to turn and find her there. She wanted to be asked a question whose answer she knew how to give.

Near the door, a group parted to let an elderly man through. Mira stepped into the space behind him and went outside. No one called her back.

Home, blessedly, was quiet.

Mira slipped inside and closed the door on the voices drifting from the Zenithian Hall. Woodsmoke clung to the aged timbers, mingling with the scent of paper and worn leather from her father’s collection. Dried herbs hung beneath the rafters. She breathed their earthy fragrance and stood a moment with her hand still upon the latch.

Elias sat in his usual corner by the window, absorbed in a thick, leather-bound volume. Late afternoon light warmed the weathered lines of his face and lay across the open pages. Outside, Rivenglade was learning Lucien’s prayers; here, her father still bent over the old books. Mira had scarcely realized how much she needed to find him doing something familiar.

“Father?”

He looked up and smiled. “Mira. Back already?”

She nodded. He glanced at her dress, then at her face, and the smile softened.

“Did you have a pleasant afternoon?”

Mira tried to think of something to tell him. Kat had been pleased she came. The sermon had ended. There had been nothing so terrible that she ought to be standing in the doorway unable to answer.

“Why am I so different from everyone else?”

Elias closed the book around one finger. “What happened?”

He waited while she came farther into the room. Beside his chair stood a stool, and she drew it nearer before sitting. The hem caught beneath one foot. She freed it and folded her hands over her knees.

“I never know what to say. Everyone else seems to know when to laugh, when to speak, how to stand there without thinking about it.” She looked up at him. “Why can’t I do that? Why do I never fit anywhere?”

“Oh, little starling.”

The name almost undid her. He reached out and cupped her cheek, his thumb resting beneath her eye.

“There is nothing wrong with you.”

“You would say that.”

“Yes,” he said. “I would.”

She leaned into his palm despite herself. His hand was warm, the skin rough where it touched her jaw. For a moment she could sit quietly and let him hold her face as he had when she was small.

“You are perfect, exactly as you are,” he said. “In every way that matters.”

She wanted to believe him, to let the afternoon settle around them until the gathering felt small and far away. But tomorrow she would meet those people in the street. Kat would ask her to attend another sermon, and Mira would go, hoping this time to find whatever came so easily to everyone else.

“But I want to fit in,” she said. “I want more friends. I want to walk into a room and have someone be glad I came, without needing Kat to bring me over.” She swallowed. “I want to be liked. To be… normal.”

Elias lowered his hand. “You needn’t become someone else to deserve that.”

“I know. But knowing doesn’t help when I’m standing there.”

Outside, a bird landed upon the oak branch. It bent beneath the small weight, then lifted as the bird hopped nearer the trunk. Mira watched it disappear among the leaves.

“Everything I love feels wrong there,” she said. “The forest, the sea, the old stories. I listen to them talk, and I think of something I’d like to tell Kat, but I already know what she’ll say about it.” Her fingers tightened together. “It’s like speaking a language no one else remembers.”

“Those things haven’t changed because people speak differently of them.”

“Kat has.”

Elias looked toward the window. He still held the book closed around his finger.

“I don’t think she means to hurt me,” Mira added. “She wants me to be happy.”

“And are you?”

Mira shook her head. For a while, they listened to the leaves stirring outside. She loosened her clasped fingers and smoothed the dress across her knees.

“What do you think Mother would say to me?” Her voice softened. “If she were here now?”

Her father grew still. His gaze dropped to the book upon his knee, though it remained closed.

Mira waited. She knew how easily a question about her mother could become a silence that lasted until supper, until morning, until she stopped asking. This time, she stayed beside him.

“She would say the same thing, little starling.” His voice had roughened. “That you are perfect. Just as you are.”

Mira looked down at his hand against the worn leather. She wanted to imagine another hand there, another voice offering the same reassurance. Yet there was so little from which to make a person. Whenever she tried, she found only the space her mother should have occupied.

“Then why did she leave?”

Elias looked up.

“Why didn’t she want me? Why didn’t she stay and raise me?”

His jaw tightened. She saw him retreat from her before he spoke, the familiar stillness settling over his face.

“She simply couldn’t, Mira.”

Mira waited for more. Outside, a bird alighted on the sill, scratched once against the wood, and flew away.

“Why couldn’t she?”

Her father said nothing.

“Please.” She tried to keep her voice steady. “You always stop there. You tell me she couldn’t stay, or that it’s better this way, and then I’m supposed to stop asking.”

“Mira—”

“But I don’t know anything about her.” Her voice rose despite her effort. “She’s my mother, and I don’t even know why she left. Why can’t you give me a real answer?”

He drew the book closer against his knee.

“Why is it always better this way?” Tears blurred her sight, and she blinked them back. “Better for whom? Because—”

“Because she’s not like us, Mira!”

He rose so abruptly that the book slid from his knees and struck the floor. She flinched. For a moment they both looked at it, lying open where it had fallen.

“She walks a different path,” he said. “A path we can never follow.”

Mira lifted her eyes to his face. He was breathing harder, one hand still held away from his side as though he had meant to catch the book. She had wanted him to say more. Now he had, and she could make no more sense of it than of everything he had refused to say.

“What does that mean?”

Elias’s hand fell. He looked at her for a long moment, but no answer came.

Mira tipped her head back and looked at the aged beams overhead. She blinked until their edges steadied, breathing carefully through the tightness in her throat. She had no strength left to ask again.

“Fine,” she whispered. “All right, Father.”

She rose, drawing the stool back with her legs. The sound scraped through the quiet room.

“Mira…”

She waited. He bent to retrieve the book, smoothing a page that had folded beneath its cover. When he straightened, she was already turning away.

In her room, she closed the door and stood with her hand upon the latch. She could still smell the incense in her sleeves. She rubbed the fabric between her fingers, then let it fall.

The small mirror hung upon the wall, clouded with age and split by a narrow crack. Mira stepped before it. Her dark hair had come loose around her face; beneath it, her violet eyes looked brighter for the tears she had been trying to hold.

She leaned nearer. There was still a faint mark upon her cheek.

Mira wet her thumb and rubbed until the skin reddened. The dirt came away easily. She had thought it would be a relief, but the face looking back was the same one she had carried through the hall and brought home to her father.

Who am I?

The tears spilled over. She let them run, watching her reflection blur until she could no longer make out the place she had cleaned.

Beyond the door, a floorboard creaked beneath her father’s step. Mira turned toward it and waited. She could picture him there with the book in his hands, searching for something to say.

She kept her hand away from the latch.

After a while, the boards creaked again. His footsteps moved down the passage, and she stood listening until they stopped.
