By morning, Mira could scarcely bear to stay indoors. Cool air stirred through Rivenglade, carrying the scent of the forest down between the houses. Beyond the rooftops, sunlight touched the highest branches. She pulled on her tunic and trousers, worked her hair into a loose braid, and went to find Kat.

Zenithian hymns drifted from a neighboring cottage as she hurried barefoot over the cobblestones. At Kat’s door, she knocked and shifted impatiently from one foot to the other, listening for movement within.

The door opened. Kat took one look at her face and folded her arms, a smile already betraying her attempt at severity.

“Oh boy. What are you up to now, Mira Roehart?”

Mira grinned and caught her wrist.

“Wait! Wait!” Kat stumbled onto the path, laughing as she pulled free. “Honestly, Mira!”

She disappeared inside before Mira could answer. When she returned, a worn leather satchel hung from her shoulder. She fastened the door, turned, and found Mira’s hand waiting for hers.

Their fingers intertwined. This time, Kat came willingly.

They ran beneath the trees with their laughter following them, startling birds from the low branches. Wind loosened Mira’s braid and tugged at Kat’s carefully arranged curls. The earth yielded beneath Mira’s feet, soft with fallen leaves, and she lengthened her stride as the path opened ahead. When she looked back, Kat was laughing too hard to finish whatever complaint she had begun.

“You know,” Kat managed at last, “you’re going to get us into serious trouble one day, running headlong into who knows what!”

“It’ll be worth it. I promise!”

They followed the familiar path until it narrowed beneath encroaching ferns, then went farther. The canopy thickened overhead. Morning light reached the ground in scattered patches, illuminating roots and the pale undersides of leaves turned by their passing. The air cooled against Mira’s face, rich with damp earth and the sweet decay of old wood.

Kat slowed beside her. Somewhere ahead, beneath the rustle of the branches, water sounded.

They stopped to listen. The low murmur grew clearer as they followed it around a bend, becoming a steady rush beyond a curtain of tangled vines. Mira could feel it faintly through the soles of her feet, as though the forest were breathing beneath them.

“Look,” she said, drawing Kat closer. “Look what I found.”

She parted the vines.

Water spilled down a craggy cliff face in a silver ribbon, breaking against dark stone before plunging into the pool below. Sunlight caught the spray and scattered across the water like fragments of glass. Along the shaded banks, moss grew thick over the rocks, its green deepened almost to black where the mist settled.

A great boulder stood at the pool’s edge. Water beaded upon its surface and gathered in the shallow hollows before slipping down to the bank. Beyond it, the pool darkened beneath the overhanging trees, its depths hidden even where the surface cleared.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

Then Mira laughed and stepped through the vines.

“Come on!”

She pulled off her tunic and trousers and left them upon a sun-warmed patch of moss. Kat stopped at the edge of the clearing, her eyes moving from Mira to the falls.

“Oh no. No, no, no. This one is all you.” She pointed toward the boulder. “I’ll just—stay here.”

“You have no sense of adventure, Katalina Petari!”

“I came with you, didn’t I?”

Kat settled upon the broad stone and opened her satchel. By the time Mira reached the rocks beside the pool, her friend had drawn out parchment and charcoal. She rested the parchment across her knees, studying the cliff with her head tilted.

Mira climbed above the bank, finding footholds between the slick folds of moss. Spray cooled her legs. From the outcrop, she could see the water spreading in pale currents from the foot of the falls before deepening beneath her.

Kat looked up.

“Mira—”

With a triumphant cry, she leapt.

Cold closed over her head. For a moment there was only the rush of water and the pale, wavering light above her. She kicked toward it and broke the surface, gasping, hair plastered across her face.

Kat was leaning forward on the boulder, her charcoal held motionless above the page.

Mira pushed the hair from her eyes and laughed.

“It’s so beautiful!” She turned in the water to look up at the cascade. “I can’t believe we’ve lived in Rivenglade our whole lives and never found this place!”

Kat sat back, shaking her head, but her smile remained.

Mira rolled onto her back. The pool held her beneath a patchwork of leaves and sky, the falls filling her ears until she could hear nothing of the world beyond the clearing. She let her arms drift wide. There was no cloth pulling at her waist, no bench confining her knees. Water slipped over her skin, cold enough to make her feel every part of herself.

When she swam back toward the bank, Kat had begun to draw.

Mira caught the edge of a submerged rock beside the boulder and rested there, watching the charcoal move. A few lines had already given shape to the cliff, though Kat kept looking up as if the next glance might show her how to catch the water.

“It is beautiful,” Kat said. Her eyes moved over the pool. “Truly… an honor, isn’t it? To witness such wonders among Zenith’s creations. We are so blessed.”

Mira glanced up through the canopy, then lowered her gaze before Kat could catch her expression. For a little while, she had forgotten the Hall entirely.

“They say the Wild Gods once roamed these forests,” she said. She let her voice soften, inviting Kat into the old stories. “That nymphs still watch over hidden pools like this one. That dryads whisper secrets through the trees.”

Kat’s charcoal stopped.

“If they do linger, may Zenith’s Light drive them out.”

Mira looked at her.

“They’re demonic creatures,” Kat continued. Her fingers tightened upon the parchment. “They lead people away from the truth. It would be better if they were cleansed from these places.”

The water moved gently against Mira’s shoulders. She had expected a smile, perhaps a teasing glance toward the trees.

“You didn’t always talk like this.”

“That was before.”

“We both believed in them, Kat. We left offerings for the forest spirits. We made flower crowns for the nymphs and whispered wishes to the dryads in the oldest oaks.”

“I remember.” Kat looked down at her drawing. “But that was before I found the truth.”

Mira waited. Kat brushed a loose fleck of charcoal from the page, then lifted her eyes again.

“Zenith’s Light makes sense. More than anything else ever has. Don’t you want that? Something you can be certain of?”

“I want to understand it.”

“Then think of what he’s offering.” Kat leaned forward. “A world without war. Without people being driven from their homes, without all that suffering. Don’t you want peace, Mira?”

“Of course I do. Who wouldn’t?”

“Then why do you always sound as though there’s something wrong with it?”

Mira loosened her hold upon the rock and looked toward the falls. Water struck the pool hard enough to throw spray against the stone, yet here beside the bank it scarcely troubled her.

“I don’t think there’s anything wrong with wanting it,” she said. “But the world has balance. Light and shadow, joy and sorrow, creation and destruction. They exist together. Too much of either creates its own chaos.”

Kat shook her head.

“There’s no such thing as too much good. Too much light. That isn’t possible.” She held Mira’s gaze. “Even a single drop of darkness is too much in this world.”

Mira could hear Lucien in the certainty of it. She had heard those words spoken over rows of attentive faces, and now Kat sat among the moss and sunlight, waiting for her to agree.

She pushed away from the rock and waded toward her clothes. The air chilled her as she left the water. She stepped into her trousers, drawing them slowly over her wet skin, trying to find an answer that would not turn the morning into another argument.

“A world without suffering is a beautiful dream,” she said. “I understand why you want to believe in it.”

“It could be more than a dream.”

Mira drew her tunic over her head. Through the fabric, Kat’s voice grew muffled, then clear again.

“For all of us. For the whole world, if we truly believed.”

Mira eased the tunic away from her damp shoulders and returned to the boulder. Kat moved her satchel to make room. The small, familiar gesture eased something in Mira, and she sat beside her, close enough to see where the charcoal had darkened the water beneath the cliff.

Kat set the drawing aside.

“Why don’t you become one of the Chosen?”

Mira looked up.

“Why don’t you do the baptism ritual?” Kat took her hands. “Just try it. Please. Be one of us.”

Her fingers were warm around Mira’s chilled ones. Mira looked down at their joined hands, at the charcoal smudged along Kat’s thumb.

“I don’t know if that’s what I truly want.”

“You won’t know until you try.”

“But I don’t feel it, Kat. That pull, that certainty everyone else seems to feel.” She hesitated. “I sit there listening, and then everyone stands up and speaks as though something wonderful has happened. I don’t know what I’m supposed to say.”

“You don’t have to know what to say.”

“I feel as though I do.” Mira glanced at her. “Honestly? It all feels so… fake.”

Kat’s smile faltered.

“To me,” Mira added. “I know it doesn’t to you.”

“Because I’ve felt it.” Kat held her hands a little more firmly. “Zenith will take away that doubt. He sees into every heart. He knows what you’re afraid of, what you need. But you have to be willing to open yourself to Him.”

Mira watched a bead of water slip from the end of her hair onto their hands. Kat brushed it away with her thumb.

“I’ve felt such peace since my baptism,” she continued. “Such purpose. All those things I used to worry about don’t trouble me the way they did.” She searched Mira’s face. “It could change you too. I know it could.”

Change her.

Mira looked out over the pool. Only a few minutes ago, she had been floating beneath the trees with no wish to be anywhere else. Kat had laughed when she surfaced. They had found this place together, and for a while it had felt as though nothing stood between them.

She wanted more mornings like this. She wanted to tell Kat what she saw in the forest without watching her friend grow still at the names she used.

“I don’t know,” she said.

“Then talk to Lucien. You don’t have to decide everything now.” Kat’s voice gentled. “Tell him what you’re feeling. He has a way of making things clearer.”

Mira glanced at the drawing beside them. Kat had left the upper branches unfinished, their lines reaching into the blank parchment. She had stopped working to listen to her, and she was still waiting, still holding her hands.

Perhaps Mira had never given Lucien a fair chance to answer. She had listened from the benches, lost the thread of his sermons, and gone home with questions she had asked everyone except him.

She could speak to him. She could do that much.

“All right, Kat. I’ll talk to Lucien.” She paused. “Maybe you’re right.”

Kat smiled and squeezed her hands before releasing them.

Mira leaned back on her arms and settled her eyes on the canopy above while her friend picked up the charcoal again. For a while, they sat without speaking. The falls poured silver over the dark cliff, and mist drifted across the boulder, cooling the places where Kat’s hands had warmed hers.

She had meant the promise. She would speak to him.

It was the rest she could not be certain of.
