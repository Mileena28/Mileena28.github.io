“I beheld the heavens emptied of their stars, and the gods upon their knees. Yet it was not the darkness that frightened me. It was the knowledge that something had been waiting within it, long before the first star was born.”

— The Last Vision of Seer Vaelith, recorded before her death

For three days, Kaelen R’Veyne had followed the Ridgeback Stalker across the Godscarred Wastes. On the fourth morning, its trail disappeared into silence.

The Wastes were never quiet. Wind rasped through canyons of petrified bone and moaned beneath the skeletal arches of long-dead gods. Molten rivers gurgled in ravines, thick and luminous as fresh-spilled ichor. Somewhere beyond sight, Beastborn roared and shrieked their challenges at the empty heavens. That morning, every sound faltered before it reached him.

Kaelen stopped atop a ridge of black glass, one hand resting on the worn leather hilt of his sword. Wind stirred the silver hair at his cheek, but its voice had fallen to a thread. Below, the land fractured into jagged peaks and narrow canyons. Nothing moved among them. Even the molten rivers had sunk to a muted hiss.

He crouched beside the Stalker’s last print. Four claw marks scored the dust, each as long as his hand, but the impression ended at the edge of bare stone. No drag marks. No displaced grit. No scent beyond the faint musk already fading in the heat. The creature had not vanished. It had changed its route, doubled back, or found stone firm enough to hide its passage. Everything left a trail. Kaelen had built his life upon that certainty. Yet the Wastes offered him nothing.

He rose and searched the horizon. Any other morning, the cry of a circling Skywraith or the far-off bellow of a Gorefang would have told him where the larger predators moved. Now there was only the diminished wind and, beneath it, an absence that pressed against his senses. Then something moved inside his mind.

Awake.

Kaelen studied the word the way he studied an unfamiliar print: without moving, and without deciding anything yet. It had carried no sound, yet he had understood it as clearly as if someone had spoken beside him. It belonged to no language he knew. There had been no inflection, no emotion—only an authority so ancient that some buried part of him recognized it. Nothing followed.

Voices were an old hazard of the Godscarred Wastes, common as thirst. Every Ashborn child knew the stories: dead gods muttering beneath the earth, poisoned winds carrying memories that did not belong to those who heard them, relic hunters wandering into the deep wastes after voices that promised impossible treasures. Kaelen had always dismissed such tales as warnings given fangs to frighten the foolish. In years of hunting he had heard the wind build half-words in the canyons and never once found a mouth.

But the voices in the stories always offered something—treasure, worship, company in the dark. This one had offered nothing. It had given its single word and gone, as though obedience were assumed.

Isolation. Heat. The residue of divine blood beneath the land. Any of them could explain a word imagined in silence. He drank from his waterskin, marked the sun above the ridgeline, and turned back to the trail.

Three mornings earlier, the Ashborn market had been loud enough to bury thought. It filled a shallow basin beneath the exposed ribs of a fallen Titan. Hide awnings stretched from bone to bone, sheltering tables crowded with salvaged armor, god-stones, smoked meat, and relics of questionable origin. Traders haggled celestial fragments against coin and better wares, each swearing to a provenance the other doubted, while children chased one another through the dust. Somewhere among the tents, a hammer rang against metal, and an old woman sang a war song badly enough that three neighboring merchants shouted competing verses to drown her out. Kaelen had gone there for crawler hide, waxed sinew, and silver rivets.

The hide merchant watched him examine a pair of finished bracers laid across her table. She was broad-faced and sun-darkened, with three silver teeth that flashed whenever she scented a sale.

“Ridgeback hide,” she said. “Twice cured.” She took up a skinning knife and dragged its edge across one bracer, hard, then held the unmarked leather toward him. “Strong enough to turn a Harpy’s talons.”

Kaelen ignored the knife and pressed his thumb against the leather. The surface had been scored and stained to resemble the dark grain of Ridgeback hide, but the material yielded too easily.

“Crawler,” he said.

“Crawler!” The merchant pressed a hand to her chest as though the word had struck her. “That is premium Ridgeback, hunter. Taken off a bull the length of two wagons. My own cousin lost a finger to the skinning.”

Kaelen looked at the hide a moment longer. Then only his eyes moved, lifting to her face. “Unusual coloring for a Ridgeback,” he said. “Common for a crawler.”

The merchant said nothing. Somewhere behind her, the armorer’s hammer filled two full strokes.

“A particularly vicious crawler,” she said at last, animated again before the words finished. She turned the bracer to catch the light. “And cured twice all the same. Waxed against rot. Stitched double at every seam. This will outlive the man wearing it.”

He turned one bracer over. The inner seam wandered toward the wrist, where the first hard impact would split it open. “The stitching is crooked.”

“The stitching has character.”

“It will tear.”

“Everything tears if a Beastborn wants it badly enough.” She leaned across the table and lowered her voice. “Fortunately, I also have something for that.”

From beneath a folded pelt, she produced a cloudy shard bound in copper wire. It might once have belonged to a larger relic. More likely, someone had chipped it from the ribs above them.

“A fragment of a goddess’s tear,” she said. “Wear it near the heart and no Beastborn will smell your fear.”

“Then keep it.”

Her silver teeth appeared again. “I have no fear.”

“You have guards.”

A bark of laughter escaped the armorer at the neighboring stall. The merchant silenced him with a look before turning back to Kaelen.

People had begun to notice him. Two boys peered around a rack of cured pelts, one pretending to wield a sword while the other crouched on all fours and snarled. Their mother caught them staring and pulled them back to her stall, though not before the smaller child whispered, “Silver Hunter.” The name moved differently through Ashborn mouths than it did in the border settlements. Here it carried pride, suspicion, and the particular wariness reserved for a man who had left them, yet kept returning whenever the Wastes demanded him.

The merchant set the charm aside. Her hands grew still upon the folded pelt, and the brightness in her face dimmed.

“You heading toward the salt ravine?”

“Why?”

“Western camp sent word this morning. Two hunters dead. A third missing.” She glanced past him, toward the western road. “They found tracks too deep for any common Ridgeback.”

“An old one.”

“So they fear.” Her eyes returned to him. “Is that what you mean to hunt?”

Kaelen tested the strength of a coil of sinew. “If I find its trail.”

She drew the untreated crawler hide closer and began assembling the materials he had selected.

“How much?” he asked.

“For you? Coin, if you carry any.” Her gaze dropped to the pouch at his belt. “Or whatever you have that once had teeth.”

Kaelen loosened the pouch and withdrew two curved talons, each as long as a finger. Razorwing. The merchant’s attention sharpened. Half the market was crawler dressed as Ridgeback and rib chips sold as goddesses’ tears, but what the Silver Hunter laid on a table had died at his feet.

He placed the talons side by side. The merchant reached immediately for the larger one. Kaelen let her take it, then closed his fingers around the second and started returning it to his pouch.

“Both,” she said.

“One.”

“One buys the hide.”

“One bought the hide, the sinew, and a pouch of rivets last season.” The second talon disappeared into his pouch. “From you.”

“Last season the southern caravans still came through.” She turned the talon between her fingers. “No caravan means no new silver. No new silver means these cost more.”

Kaelen lifted one rivet from the open pouch beside her scales. He drew the edge of his knife across its head. The shallow cut gleamed white beneath the tarnish. Genuine silver.

“How many?” he asked.

“Five.”

“Ten.”

Her eyebrows rose. “For one talon?”

“For a Razorwing talon.”

“Six.”

“Nine.”

“Seven.”

“Eight.”

She looked from the talon to Kaelen, searching his expression for room he had not given her. Finding none, she pulled the hide and sinew toward her and counted eight rivets onto the cloth.

“You’re hard to bargain with, Silver Hunter.”

“Gorefangs are easier to deal with.”

The neighboring armorer laughed again. This time the merchant allowed herself a low chuckle as she knotted the bundle and pushed it across the table.

“The western camp has posted a bounty,” she said. “They want the head as proof.”

Kaelen secured the bundle beneath his cloak. “Then they will have certainty.”

He left before she could turn the bounty into another negotiation. The two boys followed him to the edge of the market, continuing their mock battle until their voices disappeared behind him. By sundown, his mended armor sat close against his shoulders, and the Ridgeback’s trail lay beneath his boots.

Now, on the fourth morning, the memory of the market’s clamor made the silence around him feel deeper. Attachments distracted. Reputation fed him. Work stayed honest.

Voices in the silence did not.

By midday, he recovered the Ridgeback’s trail where a smear of black blood marked an outcrop. The beast had scraped its armored flank against the rock before turning west into a labyrinth of narrow canyons. Kaelen followed, keeping to the shade where he could, though heat radiated from the walls and gathered beneath his armor.

The passage tightened around him. A sweet, rotten stench overwhelmed the Stalker’s musk. Black residue slicked the canyon walls, gleaming with oily colors whenever the light touched it. It had gathered in the seams of the stone and hardened there, but Kaelen recognized it from the places his people refused to walk.

Divine blood.

The old spill had poisoned everything around it. No scrub grew between the rocks. No insects crawled through the dust. Ahead, a stagnant pool reflected the canyon in a skin of black iridescence.

Kaelen drew his sword.

The Ridgeback’s tracks continued past the pool, but new gouges scarred the wall above him. Talons. Fresh ones. He looked up as the Razorwing Harpy dropped from the canyon rim.

Its scream struck hard enough to rattle his teeth. Wings filled the narrow passage, shedding barbed feathers as it folded into the dive. Kaelen threw himself aside, and talons raked sparks from the stone where his skull had been. The Harpy climbed, twisted, and came for him again.

There was no room to evade its full reach. Kaelen raised his sword and turned the first talon, but the second caught his upper arm. Leather split. Claws tore through flesh and dragged him from his feet. Pain flashed white across his vision. He hit the ground, rolled, and drove his blade upward. The point glanced from the Harpy’s breastbone. One vast wing hammered him into the canyon wall and drove the breath from him. His sword fell from his hand and skidded toward the black pool.

The creature landed between him and the weapon. Its hooked beak opened. Ropes of dark saliva stretched between serrated edges, and the wound in Kaelen’s arm throbbed in answer to the corrupted blood surrounding them.

Heat rushed through his veins. Not the dry heat of the Wastes, but a living fire that spread from the wound and sank into muscle and bone. His heartbeat struck once, hard enough to blur the canyon. Then again.

The Harpy lunged. A roar tore from Kaelen’s throat, and his body answered it.

Muscles seized and swelled beneath his armor. Bones ground against one another, shifting with a pain so complete that the world narrowed to flashes: black stone, beating wings, his own hands braced in the dust. His fingers lengthened. Nails split and darkened into claws. Coarse fur pushed through the skin of his forearms as the veins beneath filled with cold, silver light.

Kaelen stared. The Harpy hesitated, its wings beating once in confusion. In the stagnant pool beyond it, a distorted figure crouched where Kaelen should have been. Its jaw had lengthened around rows of fangs. Silver eyes burned from a face caught between man and beast. Ashborn war songs had given that face a hundred names. Curse. Abomination. The monster waiting at the end of every tale about divine blood.

Bloodmarked.

The truth should have terrified him. Instead, power flooded every limb, terrible in its simplicity. The Harpy’s smallest movements became visible: the tightening of its talons, the shiver passing through damaged feathers, the pulse fluttering beneath the hide of its throat.

It screamed and launched itself at him. Kaelen met it in the air. His claws closed around its neck, and momentum drove them both into the canyon wall, but the impact barely reached him. He tore downward. Feathers scattered. Flesh opened beneath his hands. When the Harpy struck at him, he caught one wing and wrenched until bone broke through skin. The creature shrieked. Kaelen bit into its throat.

Hot blood filled his mouth. He tasted metal, rot, and beneath them both, the old divinity the creature carried. The Harpy thrashed beneath him, talons carving trenches through the dust. He tightened his jaws until cartilage gave way and its scream collapsed into a wet rattle. Still he did not release it. He tore until the creature stopped moving. Until the canyon floor had become a ruin of blood and feathers. Until there was nothing left to fight and the rage went on searching for something to kill.

Kaelen staggered away from the carcass. His altered reflection waited in the black pool. For one suspended moment, the creature in the water looked more truthful than the human face he remembered.

Then the power withdrew. Agony folded him. Bones contracted; claws split and receded; fur sank back beneath shuddering skin. When it ended, he knelt amid the straps and plates of his ruined armor, one hand pressed to the earth, the other slick with blood. His breath came in raw, uneven pulls.

The canyon had fallen silent again. Into that silence came the voice.

Accept.

The word entered him more deeply than the first.

You are one of us now, though you deny it.

Kaelen bowed his head, silver hair falling across his face.

You swore to destroy monsters, and yet … you wear their skin well.

He looked at his hands. Human again. Bloodied still. Beyond the canyon, the Ridgeback Stalker’s trail still ran west. He had followed it for three days believing it was the most dangerous creature in the Wastes. The Silver Hunter had spent his life following monsters.

Now one waited beneath his skin.
